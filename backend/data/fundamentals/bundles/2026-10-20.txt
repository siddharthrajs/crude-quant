EU weakens call for ban on Arctic oil and gas drilling
EU removes call for multilateral drilling ban
Continues to call for oil, gas to be left in ground
Follows Norwegian lobbying, Ukraine, Iran wars
The European Union has watered down calls for a ban on Arctic oil and gas drilling, following intense lobbying from key supplier Norway, but continues to call for hydrocarbons to be left in the ground, according to an updated strategy document.
In its previous plan, the 2021 Joint Communication on the EU Arctic Policy, the EU said it would pursue an international ban on new drilling in polar regions for environmental reasons and expressed direct opposition to oil and gas developments in the Arctic.
While its long-awaited update released Oct. 20 continues to call for Arctic oil, coal and gas to "stay in the ground" and for the region to develop renewable energy and "sustainable minerals", it drops calls to "work with partners toward a multilateral legal obligation not to allow any further hydrocarbon reserve development in the Arctic...nor to purchase such hydrocarbons if they were to be produced."
The tweak follows increasingly vocal comments from Norwegian officials, both in government and state-run Equinor, about the importance of exploration in its Arctic waters for Europe's energy security.
In August, Equinor CEO Anders Opedal told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, that the EU was taking the "wrong direction" when it comes to Arctic drilling.

US sanctions exemption unlikely to affect Russian crude output
Trump halts some restrictions, citing `national interests'
Russia is producing below the OPEC+ quota
Sanctions relief narrows discounts on Russian crude
US President Donald Trump's move to waive some sanctions targeting Russia's energy sector is unlikely to affect its crude output, which has fallen in 2026 amid massive attacks on its refining sector.
Analysts see these attacks as having a greater impact on Russian crude output, which slipped from 9.23 million b/d in January to 8.8 million b/d in September, according to the Platts OPEC+ crude production survey by S&P Global Energy. This is well below its OPEC+ quota.
Trump signed an executive order Oct. 16, waiving measures included in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, citing "national interests," days before it was due to come into effect.
The legislation targeted Russian hydrocarbons buyers, allowing the president to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported from the five largest buyers of Russian crude oil or natural gas by total volume over the year before enactment, among other measures.
Tatiana Mitrova, a fellow at Columbia University's Center on Global Energy Policy, said the waiver is unlikely to have much immediate impact on Russian crude production or export levels.
"Since it just suspends new sanctions and tariffs under the Graham Act while leaving all the current restrictions in place, it isn't a game changer," she said.

Asia, Middle East butane premium to propane hits 4-month high on tighter supply
Middle East risks support butane prices
Winter gasoline blending lifts butane demand
The butane premium over propane in Asia and the Middle East widened to more than four-month highs at the Oct. 19 market close, as strong seasonal demand for winter gasoline blending and supply disruptions in the Middle East tightened butane availability.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed CFR North Asia propane cargoes at $977.50/metric ton and butane cargoes at $1,057.50/mt on Oct. 19. Propane rose $12.50/mt from the previous session, while butane increased $17.50/mt.
The spread between CFR North Asia propane and butane cargoes was assessed at minus $80/mt on Oct. 19, compared with minus $75/mt in the previous session, as market participants cited tightening butane supply. The spread was previously wider at minus $90/mt on June 2.
Two Asia-based market sources attributed the butane premium to stronger seasonal demand from the West, where it is blended into winter-grade gasoline. Ongoing geopolitical tensions in the Middle East have also constrained butane availability from the Persian Gulf.
A regional LPG broker said the butane market had remained tight for the past two to three weeks, with availability showing limited signs of easing in the near term.
A second broker said geopolitical disruptions and winter gasoline blending demand were tightening butane supply, making evenly split LPG cargoes less attractive to importers due to their greater exposure to higher-priced butane.

Middle East risks persist Physical LPG values in the Middle East continue to be
influenced by maritime security incidents in the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab Strait.
Platts assessed FOB AG propane cargoes at $727/mt and butane cargoes at $784/mt at the Oct. 19 close, with both prices reaching a 19-week high. AG propane was last higher at $739/ mt on June 8 and AG butane was previously higher at $805/mt on June 4.
Platts assessed the spread between FOB Arab Gulf propane and butane cargoes at minus $57/mt at the Oct. 19 market close, compared with minus $52/mt on Oct. 16. The spread was previously wider at minus $60/mt on May 29.
Four ship attacks were reported between Oct. 16 and Oct. 18, including a rocket-propelled grenade attack on a tanker near Jubail on Saudi Arabia's east coast, according to the UK Maritime Trade Operations Centre.
Such incidents increase freight risks and costs for vessels transiting key LPG trade routes from the Persian Gulf, tightening an already limited butane supply in Asia and leading buyers such as India to source cargoes from more distant origins such as the US.
According to an Oct. 19 report by S&P Global Energy CERA, the near-term outlook for US butane demand remains strong, supported by expectations of higher exports to the East and increased domestic gasoline blending demand during the winter season.
"India has issued a 2027 buy tender for about 2.75 million mt of LPG. That would translate to about four Very Large Gas Carriers transporting evenly split LPG cargoes," the CERA report said.
"India has increased its import requirements to make up for the Middle East supply disruption. The main alternative supplier is the US. The Indian requirement will add to the already robust US butane exports of more than 600,000 b/d since the onset of the Middle East war," the report said.

CHINA DATA: LPG imports fall 21% MOM in Sept, tightening PDH feedstock
PDH utilization slips amid tighter propane availability
Middle East share shrinks as alternative supplies rise
China's LPG imports extended their month-over-month decline in September after reaching a 12-month high in July, tightening propane feedstock availability for the country's propane dehydrogenation sector, detailed data released Oct. 20 by the General Administration of Customs showed.
China imported 2.19 million metric tons of LPG, comprising propane and butane, in September, down 21.3% from August and 27.9% from a year earlier.
The decline followed a 12-month high of 3.3 million mt in July and has constrained feedstock availability for China's PDH plants, which rely heavily on imported propane to produce propylene.

"A plant in South China shut down for a while in early October to wait for the feedstock to arrive," an analyst with local energy information provider JLC said.
China's average PDH utilization fell to 65.49% so far in October from 71.39% in September, according to JLC.
However, operating rates could recover as plants with a combined PDH capacity of 2.01 million mt/year are scheduled to restart following maintenance, potentially boosting demand for imported propane.
Improved processing economics could also support higher operating rates. The margin for converting propane into propylene stood at 145 yuan/mt as of Oct. 18, compared with an average loss of 36 yuan/mt in September, JLC data showed.
US remains top supplier The US remained China's largest LPG supplier in September,
shipping 1.01 million mt, or about 46.3% of China's total imports. However, US volumes fell 19.4% month over month and
remained below the 15-month high of 1.35 million mt reached in July.
Over January-September, China imported 7.99 million mt of LPG from the US, down 14.1% year over year.

Houthis claim attacks on Aramco Yanbu facilities, Shaybah oil field
Houthis say fire erupted at energy sites
Early Oct Yanbu crude loadings at 3.2 mil b/d: CAS
Yemen-based Houthi militants said they targeted Saudi Arabian Oil Co. facilities in Yanbu and the Shaybah oil field with "ballistic and cruise missiles and drones," adding that fires had erupted at the sites, according to an Oct. 19 statement from the group.
Houthis targeted unspecified "Aramco facilities" in Yanbu in two phases, according to a statement by spokesperson Yahya Saree on X, claiming the attacks caused "major fires at the targeted sites." A separate operation targeting the 1 million barrels/day Shaybah oil field also resulted in "fires at the targeted sites," the statement said.
The incidents were part of a series of Houthi attacks against Saudi energy and civilian infrastructure, including an attack on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on the evening of Oct. 19, according to the statement.
Saudi authorities have yet to confirm the attacks, while Aramco did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Oct. 20.
Saudi Arabia's visible gross crude loadings over Oct. 1-10 averaged 7.1 million b/d, according to preliminary data from S&P Global Commodities at Sea, as Yanbu exports recovered following

a pipeline attack that disrupted flows in September. Visible crude loadings from Yanbu averaged 3.2 million b/d
during the period, up from a wartime low of 1.9 million b/d in September, CAS data showed.
CAS may revise the estimates as additional data becomes available. Saudi crude transported on vessels with their automatic identification systems switched off may not be fully captured in the estimates.

Gulf Keystone, ShaMaran merge to create 53,000 b/d Iraq oil producer
Deal valued at $601 million
Includes Sarsang, Atrush fields
Oil producer ShaMaran Petroleum, listed in Norway and Canada, and UK-listed Gulf Keystone Petroleum have agreed to merge to create a 53,000 barrels/day oil producer in Iraq to operate fields in the Kurdistan Region and federal Iraq, they said in an Oct. 20 statement.
The $601 million merger will combine Gulf Keystone's 61.5% Shaikan Field, one of the biggest oil fields in the Kurdistan region, with ShaMaran's non-operated positions in the Atrush Field, at 50%, and the Sarsang Field, at an 18% working interest.
"ShaMaran today announced expanding into Federal Iraq with HKN Energy in the Hamrin field," said Garrett Soden, CEO of ShaMaran and proposed non-executive director of Gulf Keystone. Hamrin was previously run by the Iraqi state-owned North Oil Company, and production is around 20,000 b/d, according to a separate statement from ShaMaran. HKN signed an agreement with North Oil Company in July to develop the field located in the Kirkuk and Salahaddin provinces and boost its output.
Jon Harris, CEO and executive director of Gulf Keystone, said in the press release that the enlarged company will provide a stronger platform to invest in the future of the Shaikan and Hamrin fields and to work with HKN on ShaMaran's assets.
The combined entity will have estimated reserves of 323 million barrels and production of 53,000 b/d of crude, ranking it among Europe's top 10 listed independent oil and gas companies, the statement said.

The Nikolskoye linear production and dispatch station, part of the Druzhba pipeline system near Michurinsk in Russia's Tambov Region, was attacked overnight on Oct. 19-20, according to several media outlets.

NORWAY DATA: Hydrocarbons output dips in maintenance-heavy September
NCS production down 10% on month
Gas, crude miss official NOD forecasts
Total liquids output at 1.9 million b/d
Norway's combined oil and gas output dipped 10% month over month in September, missing an official forecast, the country's hydrocarbons regulator said Oct. 20.
In an update on its website, the Norwegian Offshore Directorate said the Scandinavian country produced 1.9 million barrels/day of liquids in September and 298 million cubic meters/ day of natural gas. Those figures were, respectively, 1.1% and 6.7% below its forecasts.
Crude oil production alone came in at 1.73 million b/d, compared to 1.88 million b/d in August and 1.89 million b/d in the

same month of 2025. Natural gas production was 13% lower month over month, but
up 5% year over year, the NOD data showed. September was the weakest month of 2026 so far for oil and
gas production on the NCS, the NOD said. Norway's government does not outline the specific reasons
for production fluctuations, but maintenance is commonplace across its dozens of offshore oil and gas fields, with September traditionally the biggest month for turnarounds.
Despite crude prices surging on the back of disruptions to flows through the Strait of Hormuz this year, Norwegian operators including state-run Equinor made it clear they would stick to planned maintenance schedules.
The country is a linchpin oil and gas supplier for Europe, its importance having grown in the wake of the wars in Ukraine and Iran. Norwegian crude grades underpin the Platts Dated Brent benchmark, which was last assessed at $117.61/b on Oct. 19.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed the month-ahead Dutch TTF gas price at Eur87.785/MWh on Oct. 19, the highest level since December 2022.
Ship-tracking data from S&P Global Commodities at Sea shows strong Norwegian crude exports in September at 1.71 million b/d, with the Netherlands, Poland and the UK its largest customers. That is up from 1.58 million b/d in August.
Norwegian producers have been working hard to offset natural declines on the NCS by reviving shuttered fields, making new Barents Sea discoveries, and undertaking infrastructure-led exploration to extend field life. The NOD has warned that oil and gas production could start to dip after 2027 without major new investments and discoveries.

Mexico diesel shortages expose strain in Pemex-led supply system
Diesel shortages hit central, western Mexico
Pemex shoulders growing supply burden
Shortages challenge government supply assurances
Mexico's diesel distribution network is coming under pressure as refinery problems, pipeline disruptions and adverse weather compound logistical constraints at state oil and gas company Pemex, with shortages reported across parts of central and western Mexico, according to three independent market sources.
The difficulties come as Pemex has taken on a greater role supplying the domestic fuels market amid a retreat by private importers, leaving the distribution system more exposed when problems affect the state company's refining and logistics infrastructure.
Market sources confirmed fuel shortages, with one saying the Querétaro region had been without diesel for a week and that supply difficulties had also affected Guadalajara, the second largest city in Mexico.
One source attributed the disruptions to a combination of operational and logistical problems.
"Pemex's Tula refinery was out, while a pipeline supplying fuels into central Mexico had been shut after being repeatedly tapped," the person said. A second source confirmed that Tula had been out on maintenance works.
Pemex did not respond to requests for comment. Adverse weather in Veracruz has further complicated fuel movements supplying the Valley of Mexico, including Azcapotzalco, according to the source. Shipping data from S&P Global Commodities At Sea showed

intermittent gasoil and diesel discharge activity at Tuxpan, despite the port handling volumes as high as 2.5 million barrels on a 30-day pace, consistent with market reports that adverse weather has disrupted an important seaborne supply route into central Mexico.
The disruptions highlight the vulnerability of a fuel supply system increasingly reliant on Pemex, particularly when refinery and pipeline constraints occur simultaneously.
Market participants say the government's voluntary price agreement, which seeks to keep diesel below Peso 27/l ($5.55/ gal) and regular gasoline below Peso 24/l ($4.93/gal), has squeezed the economics of private fuel imports, shifting more of the supply burden to Pemex and reducing alternatives when the state company's refining or logistics system is disrupted.
Industry participants have also reported low inventories at terminals serving the Valley of Mexico, including Barranca, Añil, Azcapotzalco and San Juan, as bad weather hit the port of Tuxpan in Veracruz.
The supply difficulties come despite official assurances that fuel supplies remain guaranteed.
The energy ministry said Oct. 16 Pemex and private companies had sufficient inventories and their distribution networks were operating normally, reiterating that gasoline and diesel supplies were "guaranteed nationwide".
-- Sheky Espejo
North Dakota oil output rises as prices spur faster completions
August output climbs 2.4% month over month
DUC inventories drop as completions speed up
Three-mile laterals dominate at 56% of permits
North Dakota oil production edged higher in August, while elevated prices supported faster completions, a drawdown in drilled-but-uncompleted wells and continued adoption of longer laterals, according to the state's Department of Mineral Resources Oct. 20.
August oil production totaled 1.16 million b/d, 5.6% above the revenue forecast for the month and a 2.4% increase month over month, according to DMR's Geology Program Manager Richard Suggs who spoke during the state's monthly production webinar.
Natural gas production came in at 3.63 Bcf/d, up 0.3% from July as gas-to-oil ratios remained at record levels and is expected to continue rising, according to Justin Kringstad, director of the state's Pipeline Authority.
North Dakota's state rig count was unchanged from July to August at 34 rigs, with 19 operators actively drilling and around eight frac crews.
Suggs noted that DUC inventories were being depleted, a result of operators capitalizing on elevated oil prices. Simultaneously, spud-to-simulation time fell to around 75 days in an effort to bring production online faster and due to efficiency gains, he said.

INTERVIEW: SAF scale-up needs investor push, Dubai Airports CEO warns
Aviation's green fuel push stalls on cost and scale
eSAF could be game-changer for sun-rich Middle East
Hormuz disruption causes minimal Dubai fuel issues
The head of Dubai Airports has warned that sustainable aviation fuel will remain a marginal energy source for the global aviation industry unless a major injection of capital into production infrastructure breaks a self-reinforcing cycle of high costs and weak demand.
Dubai Airports CEO Paul Griffiths told Platts in an interview that SAF currently accounts for barely 1% of global jet fuel consumption -- which runs at around 7 million to 8 million b/d -- and that the economics of scaling up production remain deeply challenging for an airline industry operating on thin margins and already facing soaring oil prices amid the US-Israeli war with Iran.
"The only way forward is to stimulate [SAF] supply," Griffiths said. "Don't start at the demand end, start at the supply end. The biggest single problem you've got to solve is the fact that there is inadequate supply of SAF to make even the smallest dent in global jet fuel demand."
His comments reflect growing frustration among airport operators and aviation sustainability advocates that policy fragmentation, feedstock constraints and the steep cost premium of SAF are preventing the fuel from gaining meaningful traction in global aviation trade flows, despite regulatory mandates in Europe and elsewhere.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed SAF, produced via the hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids pathway CIF Northwest Europe at $2,498.75/metric ton on Oct. 19, a 52% premium to jet fuel on an equivalent basis. The premium has shrunk from 176% on Feb. 27, before the US and Israel war with Iran boosted jet fuel prices.
Analysts at S&P Global Energy Horizons expect 60,000 barrels/day of SAF production globally in 2026, rising to 72,000 b/d in 2027 and 97,000 b/d in 2028.
Supply chain fix Griffiths said only economies of scale can bring SAF costs
down to parity with conventional jet fuel, and proposed

assembling a financial consortium of capital partners to invest directly in SAF production infrastructure.
"We're not going to do this unless we produce SAF at scale, and you're not going to get investment in SAF at scale until you've got demand at scale," Griffiths said. "If demand is dependent on equalized pricing with jet A1, then you've got to invest in supply to [bring] the production cost down to the production cost of jet A1."
Griffiths leads the aviation workstream at the Sustainable Markets Initiative, a global private-sector organization for sustainable transition.
He outlined one potential mechanism to bridge the cost gap: blending SAF into conventional jet fuel at the airport level and spreading the cost premium across the entire fuel supply via a small surcharge on jet fuel.
Under a 10% blend scenario in which SAF costs twice as much as conventional fuel, the SAF premium would be distributed across the remaining 90% of the fuel supply, resulting in a modest per-unit surcharge for airlines, he said.
As blend ratios rise and production volumes increase, unit costs should fall, he added.
Griffiths acknowledged, however, that implementing such a scheme would require unanimity among fuel suppliers operating at any given airport, since SAF must be introduced into the shared fueling system rather than delivered separately to individual aircraft.
Securing that agreement in a cost-sensitive market would be difficult, he said.
Griffiths pointed to Singapore, Heathrow and Amsterdam Schiphol as airports that have moved furthest toward embedding SAF into their fuel supply chains -- through passenger ticket surcharges, government-mandated blend targets and sustainability compliance requirements. But he expressed skepticism that consumer preference for greener airlines would be sufficient on its own to drive wider adoption, particularly given current market conditions.
On the policy side, Griffiths said the absence of a universal government mandate remained the central obstacle to investment in SAF production. He noted that EU policy and US policy were "diametrically opposed" -- with the US incentivizing supply-side production without mandating consumption, while Europe had introduced consumption mandates without yet enforcing them.
"Unless more governments mandate this, the incentive to invest in the production of SAF isn't really there," he said.
He also noted that several airlines that had previously committed to SAF purchase policies and carbon offset programs had quietly abandoned those pledges "because they don't think that particular initiative is something that's economically sustainable."
At a 30% SAF blend ratio -- a level that would make a material difference to aviation's carbon footprint -- the cost premium would be so large that any airline absorbing it would become uncompetitive, Griffiths said.

projects under way but no permanent SAF blend in the airport's aviation fuel supply, and no clear near-term pathway toward one.

He described maritime decarbonization as "somehow stalled," with progress currently concentrated on improving the energy efficiency of existing ships and newer vessel designs.
Uncertainty over the International Maritime Organization's decisions had held back ordering before prompting owners to choose conventional vessels, said Dario Bocchetti, Grimaldi Group's head of energy-saving, research and design, and ship design.

Orders may not translate to demand Meanwhile, dual-fuel vessels, such as new methanol-capable
orders, may not ensure that a new ship will operate entirely on methanol, Bocchetti added.

DNV said more than 294 methanol-capable vessels were on order globally as of August 2026, making methanol the secondlargest alternative-fuel technology in the orderbook after LNG, based on its latest Maritime Forecast to 2050, released in August. The forecast puts shipping's low-greenhouse-gas fuel demand, covering low-GHG fuels collectively, at 4 million-22 million mt of oil equivalent in 2030, depending on regulatory outcomes. DNV noted that scaling production depends on confidence that demand will materialize. It also said the existing fleet and orderbook would largely determine which fuels shipping can consume in 2030.
Marine Plus president and CEO Dimitris Vranopoulos said fuel supply and trading patterns would continue to constrain owners' investment in dual-fuel vessels. "Even post-2030, it will be quite difficult and challenging," Vranopoulos said, adding that "everything has to be synchronized."
