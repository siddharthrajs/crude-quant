US EIA sharply raises oil price forecast on Middle East supply disruptions
Agency lifts Q4 Brent forecast $14/b to $105/b
Tight supply pushes US diesel above $6/gal, pressures crude
Middle East disruptions cut output 4.5 million b/d

US diesel export ban could lift prices with 'buffers' growing thin: Chevron CEO
Export curbs would send 'bad signal' to allies
Oil market fundamentals 'remain distressed'
Iraq, Venezuela offer growth opportunities

The US Energy Information Administration has sharply raised its near-term oil price forecast, projecting crude will average $105/barrel in the fourth quarter of 2026 as Middle East supply disruptions drain global inventories and tight diesel markets amplify upward price pressure.
The EIA's October Short-Term Energy Outlook marked a $14/b upward revision from last month's forecast and followed a surge in Dated Brent to an average of $114/b in September -- a $23/b jump from August's average. The agency forecasts global oil inventories will fall by a further 700,000 barrels/day in Q4 after declining by 1.9 million b/d in Q3, a trajectory that points to sustained tightness in physical crude and products markets through the end of the year.
The agency said oil and product flows from the Middle East were likely to "generally increase" due to "a combination of convoys through the Strait of Hormuz and workarounds to oil exports ... including bypass routes and increased use of shipto-ship transfers." However, the threat of more regional attacks and other market disruptions was likely to hold prices higher for longer than previously forecast.
"Although we expect oil production and flows from the Middle East will increase, we raised our global oil price forecast compared with last month," EIA said. "The attacks on the EastWest pipeline in Saudi Arabia highlight the potential for continued volatility in physical oil flows and oil amid ongoing withdrawals of oil inventories globally."

A US diesel export ban would be unlikely to reduce domestic prices and would send a "very bad signal" to America's allies, Chevron CEO Mike Wirth said Oct. 6, adding that the buffers that have cushioned the market impact of the US-Iran war are "much thinner today."
Speaking at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London following weeks of speculation about a potential ban on US diesel exports, Wirth said such a move could in fact increase diesel prices in some parts of the US.
According to reports, the Trump administration has been considering a ban amid surging diesel prices ahead of the pivotal midterm elections in November, although speculation over a ban has been tempered somewhat in recent days by a planned refined product stock release by members of the International Energy Agency.
"The issue that we are facing right now in the energy system are constraints on supply," Wirth said. "The system responds with workarounds," from the US easing marine transportation bans to Persian Gulf exporters finding new ways to get oil to markets.
"Actions that increase flexibility in the system, that increase supply are the actions that allow the market then to work. Restricting the supply, which an export ban would do, is the reverse," he added. "The US is not independent of world markets, it is interconnected. It exports diesel off the Gulf Coast, it imports diesel into the east and west coasts."

India's upstream strategy seeks to reset economics of deepwater exploration
New Delhi pledges $8.8B under National Offshore Exploration Scheme
Risk-sharing, technology to play key roles in India's offshore strategy
Government funding support to ease pressure on upstream companies
India is accelerating efforts to unlock its offshore oil and gas potential through a key policy initiative that combines public investment, financial support, and risk-sharing measures aimed at making frontier drilling commercially viable by reducing the financial burden on upstream companies, government officials, CEOs, and analysts told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, Oct. 6.
The National Offshore Exploration Scheme -- or 'Samudra Manthan' -- is designed to address one of the biggest barriers to offshore exploration: the high cost and risk of drilling wells that may not result in a commercial discovery. The initiative seeks to enhance the attractiveness of India's deepwater and ultradeepwater acreage for both domestic and international oil and gas companies, they added.
"Samudra Manthan will be a game changer for India's upstream sector. It will not only open new frontiers for oil and gas exploration but will act as a major catalyst for the country's energy security," Ranjit Rath, chairman and managing director of Oil India Ltd., told Platts.
"The program marks one of the biggest policy initiatives, as the government, recognizing the high-risk and high-cost nature of offshore exploration, has adopted a risk-sharing approach to unlock the resources," he added.
The federal Cabinet in late July approved investments of up to 840 billion Indian rupees ($8.8 billion) over five years to advance offshore oil and gas exploration under the scheme. The initiative is expected to catalyze reserve accretion of more than 600 million mt of oil equivalent by fiscal 2030-31 (April-March), the petroleum ministry said July 31.
The government will fund up to 50% of eligible deepwater exploration drilling costs, capped at $71 million per well. By partially covering the cost, the scheme can significantly improve the economics of frontier exploration. The allocation comes at a time when India has offered 18 deepwater and ultra-deepwater blocks under the 10th and 11th Open Acreage Licensing Policy rounds, or OALP-X and OALP-XI, analysts and officials said.
"Samudra Manthan is precisely the kind of risk-sharing intervention international operators respond to, because it reduces the cost of a dry hole rather than promising a larger share of a success that may never come," Arun Kumar Singh, chairman and CEO of Oil and Natural Gas Corp. Ltd., told Platts. "For an operator, this changes the arithmetic of a dry hole in the deep waters."

Petroleum Minister Hardeep Singh Puri told a conference in the week ended Oct. 2 that Samudra Manthan would bolster India's energy security by expanding exploration capabilities and unlocking nearly 1 million square kilometers of offshore areas previously classified as "No-Go" zones. The initiative is expected to strengthen domestic exploration and production while helping reduce the country's reliance on imports.
Vast unexplored potential India's most prospective yet underexplored offshore basins,
including Krishna-Godavari, Cauvery, Mahanadi, Bengal, and Andaman, require significant upfront investment before their commercial potential can be proven, S&P Global Energy analysts said. Oil India is expanding exploration across several of these basins, while ONGC's Project DeepX aims to double the company's deepwater drilling activity over the next two years, Platts reported earlier.
Analysts said a single deepwater exploration well can cost $125 million-$150 million, while ultra-deepwater wells may require even greater investment depending on water depth and geological complexity. In basins with limited drilling histories and uncertain success rates, such expenditures have often been difficult for operators to justify.
"India has made significant progress in improving its regulatory and licensing framework, but attracting greater exploration investment into frontier deepwater and ultradeepwater basins would require measures that improve exploration economics and address the high-risk, high-cost nature of offshore drilling," said Rahul Chauhan, senior principal upstream analyst at S&P Global Energy CERA.
The program's significance extends beyond the possibility of new oil and gas discoveries. It could also support the development of domestic technology, skills, and supply chains required to operate in technically demanding offshore environments, industry officials said.
Private-sector companies have also welcomed the initiative. "Samudra Manthan recognizes a simple reality -- India's deepwater resources represent one of the largest remaining opportunities to strengthen domestic energy capability," Kartikeya Dube, chairman of BP India, told Platts recently. "Its significance lies not only in the resources that may be discovered, but in the technology, skills, and ecosystem that can be developed around them."
Risk and reward Gauri Johar, Global Executive Director for Strategic Climate
and Energy Initiatives at S&P Global Energy, said India had listened to investors by addressing the risk-reward equation through the Samudra Manthan program.

Indonesia fuel import tenders draw few bidders amid supply disruption: ministry
Middle East conflict disrupts supply chains
Jakarta seeks alternative supplies at higher costs
Import sources drop 25% since conflict began
Indonesia's fuel import tenders are attracting few or no participants as the Middle East conflict disrupts regional supply chains, forcing Jakarta to seek alternative suppliers at potentially higher costs, Laode Sulaeman, director general for oil and gas at the Energy and Mineral Resources Ministry, said at a government meeting Oct. 5.
Sulaeman said that state-owned Pertamina is increasingly sourcing cargoes outside its normal tender process.
"Previously, Pertamina held tenders, but now there are relatively few participants in the tenders. So we have to look for countries that have surplus stocks," Sulaeman said.
"We may have to buy at prices that are potentially higher than what we would pay through the usual procurement process," he added.
Indonesia has lost around 25% of its oil import sources since the conflict in the Middle East began, he said, without specifying which suppliers or volumes were affected.
Singapore was Indonesia's top gasoline supplier, accounting for more than 57% of the total gasoline and blendstocks discharged into Indonesia in 2025, according to S&P Global Commodities at Sea data. So far this year, Singapore has

accounted for 67% of Indonesia's gasoline and blendstock discharges, while the country has added supplies from Latvia, Brunei and Qatar, none of which loaded gasoline cargoes discharged in Indonesia in 2025.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed Gasoline Unl 92 FOB Singapore Cargo at $146.16/barrel on Oct. 5, down $2.15/b from the previous assessment.
The Indonesian government has repeatedly sought to diversify its supply sources since hostilities began earlier this year. Energy Minister Bahlil Lahadalia said in March that Indonesia was exploring additional crude supplies from countries including the United States, Angola, Brazil and Russia after disruptions affected flows through the Strait of Hormuz. Only about 20%25% of the country's crude imports normally transit the strait, according to the minister.
Jakarta subsequently secured a commitment for 150 million barrels of Russian crude and has discussed longer-term supply arrangements with Moscow as part of broader energy security cooperation. The government is also preparing a long-term contract for Russian crude after receiving its first shipment, Lahadalia said in August.
CAS data showed that crude and condensate loadings from the Middle East Gulf region that were discharged into Indonesia averaged 81,700 b/d in 2025, accounting for 22% of total discharges. The Middle East Gulf share has averaged 31,000 b/d so far in 2026, accounting for 10% of total discharges, while loadings from South America and the Red Sea have increased to averages of 47,000 b/d and 23,800 b/d, respectively.
Fuel stocks The government is maintaining national fuel stocks equivalent
to 18-20 days of supply, Sulaeman said, adding that any queues at fuel stations were not due to a shortage of national stocks.
One factor disrupting fuel distribution was El Niño, which had dried up some rivers used to transport fuel, requiring distributors to use trucks with less capacity than river vessels, he said.
The government is also keeping the prices of subsidized diesel and gasoline, including RON 90 Pertalite, unchanged through the end of the year to help contain inflation, he said. The fuels are widely used by farmers and fishermen, making price stability important to the government.
Unsubsidized prices to remain stable Meanwhile, Pertamina Patra Niaga Planning and Business
Growth Director Joko Pranoto said prices of unsubsidized fuels and jet fuel would remain at September levels in October. Pertamina had not fully passed higher global market costs through to domestic fuel prices in 2026, he said.
He added that domestic unsubsidized fuel and jet fuel prices remained relatively competitive with prices in neighboring countries and other markets, including Southeast Asia, Australia, Pakistan and India, despite the price shocks in March and April that followed the escalation of the conflict.

China's LPG imports fall 15% MOM in September; US remains top supplier: CAS
US supplies 51% of China's September LPG imports
Iranian shipments fall nearly 60% to 161,000 mt
Canadian deliveries rise 61% month over month
China's LPG imports declined 15.07% month over month to 2.339 million metric tons in September, according to S&P Global Commodities at Sea data as of Oct. 6.
September's imports were down 26.42% year over year, marking the fifth consecutive month of weaker annual comparisons, the CAS data showed.
The US remained China's largest LPG supplier in September, shipping 1.200 million mt, down 13.42% from 1.386 million mt in August. US cargoes accounted for 51.3% of China's total LPG imports in September, up from 50.3% a month earlier, as the decline in overall inflows lifted the US market share despite lower absolute volumes.
Canada was China's second-largest LPG supplier in September, delivering 281,000 mt, up 60.57% from 175,000 mt in August. Its share of China's total LPG imports rose to 12.0% from 6.4% the previous month.
Iran's LPG shipments to China fell 59.75% month over month to 161,000 mt in September. Iran accounted for 6.9% of China's total LPG imports, down from 14.5% in August, according to the CAS data.
Middle East-origin LPG volumes totaled 344,000 mt in September, down 50.1% from 689,000 mt in August, the CAS data showed.
Propane accounted for the bulk of China's September LPG imports at 2.006 million mt, down 9.84% from 2.225 million mt in August. Butane imports fell 37.05% month over month to 333,000 mt.
Propane represented 85.8% of total China LPG imports in September, up from 80.8% in August, while butane's share declined to 14.2% from 19.2%, the CAS data showed.
Beijing excluded US crude and propane from the US-China agreement on a $30-billion tariff-reduction framework reached during the Sept. 23-25 summit between US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, according to a statement released by China's Ministry of Commerce Sept. 28, as reported earlier by Platts.
The tariffs on US crude and LPG are unlikely to be adjusted in the short term as the US and China have extended their bilateral

trade truce through Jan. 10, 2027, the report showed. Earlier, China's LPG imports fell 15.6% month over month to
2.79 million mt in August from a 12-month high in July, though volumes remained the highest for any month in 2026 so far, according to General Administration of Customs data and trade estimates, Platts reported Sept. 23.
The decline is expected to tighten feedstock availability for China's propane dehydrogenation sector, which relies heavily on imported propane to produce propylene, according to the Platts report.
JLC data showed average PDH utilization edged down 0.12 percentage point to 75.6% as of Sept. 17, although it remained above the August average of 67.1%.
Margins also deteriorated sharply. Average propane-topropylene margins fell 104% week over week to a loss of 9 yuan/ mt, according to JLC data.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed CFR North Asia propane cargoes at an average of $872.89/mt in September, up from $741.90/mt in August. CFR North Asia butane cargoes averaged $906.84/mt in September, up from $766.90/mt in August.
Platts assessed FOB Arab Gulf propane cargoes at an average of $658.55/mt in September, up from $586.15/mt in August. FOB Arab Gulf butane cargoes averaged $698.68/mt in September, up from $608.20/mt in August.

Construction of Iraq-Syria oil pipeline could begin in Q1 2027: official
Syria eyes role as regional energy corridor
Iraq seeks alternatives to Hormuz for oil exports
Damascus eyes new offshore gas in four years
Syria hopes to start construction on a new crude pipeline from its Banias port to Iraqi oil fields in the first quarter of next year, potentially opening a new route to the Mediterranean that avoids the Strait of Hormuz, the head of Syria's state oil firm said Oct. 6.
In a panel discussion at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London, Yousef Qiblawy, CEO of the Syria Petroleum Company, said a front-end engineering and design study for the planned pipeline was underway, three months after a bilateral deal between Damascus and Baghdad.
"Hopefully in Q1 next year we will start the construction," he said, adding that the new government of Ahmed al-Sharaa was keen to put "Syria back on the map as a safe energy transit country".
Iraq has been seeking alternative routes for its more than 4 million barrels/day of crude oil, having previously relied heavily on exports from Basrah port through the Strait of Hormuz to international markets.

East-West oil pipeline now flowing at 5.8 million b/d after restart: Saudi energy minister
East-West pipeline restarted days after drone attack
Pipeline flows at 5.8 million b/d on Oct. 6
Majority of Yanbu loadings not visible since Houthi threat
Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline returned to operations within "five or six days" after it was attacked and is pumping 5.8 million barrels/day of crude as of Oct. 6, according to Saudi energy minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, Saudi news outlet Al Arabiya reported the same day.
Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, with a capacity to transport up to 7 million b/d of crude oil, was shut down following Sept. 10 drone attacks, causing significant disruptions to oil supply from the Red Sea while flows from the Persian Gulf remain troubled.
"Not everyone knows that within five or six days [of the attack on the East-West pipeline] we started using the line," the Saudi energy minister told the Made in GCC conference in Bahrain Oct. 6, in comments broadcast on Al Arabiya.
The East-West pipeline is central to Saudi Arabia's contingency plans to bypass shipping disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz amid the US-Iran conflict, allowing the country to continue exporting crude worldwide amid the ongoing conflict in the Middle East.

"And today, this morning, we are back to 5.8 million b/d," Prince Abdulaziz said, noting Saudi Arabia's localization efforts -- ensuring resources for repairs are available in-country -- as the driver for the pipeline's restart within less than a week.
The Saudi energy ministry declined to comment. The East-West pipeline moves Saudi export barrels that are largely priced against Middle East sour crude benchmarks. Platts Dubai, the regional oil price reference for medium-sour crude, was assessed by Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, $105.69/b on Oct. 6, down from a recent high of $128/b on Sept. 16. Saudi crude loadings from Yanbu resumed on Sept. 23 after being suspended on Sept. 12 following the attack on the EastWest pipeline, according to ship tracker data from S&P Global Commodities at Sea. In the seven months since the war began, Yanbu loadings have averaged 2.8 million b/d, from June's 4 million b/d wartime high to 1.9 million b/d of visible shipments over September, CAS showed on Oct. 6.

TURKEY DATA: August imports of Russian crudes rise sharply from Sept
Iraqi and Kazakh import volumes fall
Saudi, Nigeria, Gabon, Brazil imports climb
Drop in Black Sea attacks coincides with rise in Russian imports
Turkey's imports of Russian crude rose sharply in August for a second month despite increased disruption to shipping in the Black Sea and as tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz faced continuing problems, Turkish government data showed.
According to new figures from Turkey's General Directorate of Mineral and Petroleum Affairs, the country's total crude imports in August fell by 3.0% to 2.844 million metric tons compared to July, and climbed 1.4% year over year.
Shipments from Russia, which was Turkey's largest source of crude until recently, jumped 26.9% from July when imports were up 56.6% from June, but were still 42.8% down year on year.
Turkey's June imports of Russian crude were the lowest in more than a decade.
August imports of Russian crude totaled 844,853 mt, of which 75.6% was Russian Export Blend imported by Turkey's main refiner Tupras. The volumes were up 74% on July and 8.6% year on year.
The balance was Urals REBCO imported by Socar's STAR refinery, down 31% on July and down 68% year over year.
Both refiners had been regular importers of large volumes of Russian crude until earlier this year, with their shift in buying strategy coinciding with the EU's announcement that it would bar imports of petroleum products refined from Russian crude.
Russian crude accounted for 29.7% of Turkey's crude imports in August, up from 22.7% in July, 16% in June and 19% in May, but down sharply from the 52.6% reported in August 2025, the data showed.

IRAQ DATA: Sep crude exports rise 25% on month as Hormuz flows rebound
Exports reach nearly 3 million b/d
Gulf terminal shipments up 321,000 b/d on month
Launched exports via Syrian port of Banias
Iraqi crude exports rose 25% month over month in September, as more Iraqi oil passed through the Strait of Hormuz, according to data from state oil marketer SOMO, obtained exclusively by Platts Oct. 5.
The data showed that exports hit 2.960 million barrels/day in September, up 583,000 b/d from August levels.
Despite the substantial increase, September flows were around 83% of the 3.568 million b/d Iraq exported in February, before shipping restrictions and attacks linked to the US-Iran war disrupted production and exports in the Middle East.
September exports included 2.571 million b/d from the southern Gulf terminals, up 321,000 b/d month over month. This was up from 2.250 million b/d in August, but is well below the February rate of 3.335 million b/d.
The rise in exports from the Gulf terminals was due to the increased number of tankers carrying Iraqi oil arriving and loading after crossing the Strait of Hormuz. On Oct. 4, Iraqi Oil Minister Basim Mohammed Khudair al-Abadi said "Iraq had endured difficult circumstances in the recent past, but the crisis is beginning to subside," according to an oil ministry statement.
Gulf terminal exports accounted for 70% of the oil passing through the Strait of Hormuz during the month, SOMO chief Ali Shatri said Sept. 3.
September Basrah Gulf terminal exports consisted of 1.968 million b/d of Basrah Medium crude and 603,000 b/d of Basrah Heavy crude. This compares to 1.799 million b/d and 451,000 b/d in August, respectively, the SOMO data showed. Exports remain below February levels of 2.120 million b/d of Basrah Medium crude and 1.215 million b/d of Basrah Heavy crude.
Basrah Medium has a nominal API gravity of 29 degrees, while Basrah Heavy carries a gravity of 23-24 API.
Iraq also increased exports via Ceyhan and the Syrian port of Banias and resumed supplies to Qayara, the data showed.
Ceyhan exports, which consisted of oil from Kirkuk and the Kurdistan region, doubled month over month to 254,000 b/d.
These volumes, pumped via the Kurdistan pipeline, increased from 180,000 b/d in July and 198,000 b/d in February.
This indicates that IOCs in Kurdistan resumed exports of around 120,000 b/d during the month. This resumption was due to attacks on the Kurdistan region ceasing in early August.
In August, all exports from Ceyhan were Kirkuk federal oil pumped through the Kurdistan pipeline, and no oil was supplied by the IOCs working in Kurdistan.

Those consumers would pay about 21% more for heating oil this winter than last, the agency expected, driven by a projected 30% increase in heating oil prices, only partially offset by a forecast of milder weather in the region.
Electricity users are also expected to face higher bills. Wholesale electricity prices are forecast to average $52 per megawatt-hour in 2026, 11% higher than in 2025, with PJM wholesale prices rising 41% -- though Northwest Mid-Columbia prices are forecast to fall 23%. Record-breaking summer temperatures drove a 4% increase in electricity consumption in Q3 2026 compared with the same period last year.
US retail gasoline prices averaged $4.35/gal in September. The EIA forecasts retail gasoline prices will average $3.91/gal in 2026 before easing to $3.56/gal in 2027, following expected declines in crude.

"What might on the very surface feel like a thing that would help US consumers, particularly when there are concerns over upcoming elections and the politics involved, might not have the desired effect," Wirth said. "You could see some upward pressure on prices in some parts of the country."
"And I think particularly for our allies it would send a very bad signal, when the US has been a reliable supplier in a time of need... we need to continue to serve that role."
Oil demand growth Addressing wider market dynamics, Wirth said Chevron has
not "fundamentally changed" its view on oil demand, despite the war in the Middle East.
He noted that "buffers", including a 5 million barrel/day reduction in Chinese oil buying, high pre-war inventories, strong supply at the start of the year and a large quantity of sanctioned oil on water that previously struggled to find a home, had helped temper price spikes.
"The reality is those buffers are much thinner today than they were when this began," the Chevron boss said. "Releases on the products side will help mitigate some of the near-term pressure, but underlying fundamentals remain distressed and the system is more fragile today than earlier on in the situation."
While it is too early to say when those buffers might run out, Wirth said, "the pressure continues to be to the upside", with the "underlying fundamentals... becoming tighter and tighter."
Indeed, the physical price of oil in Asia today is "much closer to $150/b than to $100/b," he said, noting that futures prices are "a very poor indicator" of supply-demand fundamentals.
In the long term, Wirth said energy affordability and reliability were back on the agenda for governments across the world. Energy demand will continue to be lifted, he said, by population growth, economic development, and AI's voracious electricity demand.
International opportunities While the Iran war is providing a windfall for oil and gas

As a result, additional revenue will be used for organic growth, as well as to strengthen the balance sheet to support Chevron through the cycles, he said.
Internationally, the company is still in negotiations with Iraq's government over West Qurna and Nasiriyah, both "prolific hydrocarbon regions" that were previously rendered uncompetitive for Chevron by Iraq's fiscal terms.
"The economics look better today," Wirth said. "Iraq is attractive, we have had very good engagement with the government there, and I am very optimistic that those negotiations will lead to a good deal." A result is still months away, he noted.
The company is also open to participating in new midstream infrastructure, with Baghdad eager to build a pipeline to Syria to supply crude to the Mediterranean market, which Wirth said could make Iraqi oil projects even more attractive.
While Chevron would not build such a system on its own, "we could be one part of a multi-party consortium that could do something like that," he said.
In addition, Wirth was bullish on the prospects for Venezuela, where Chevron was operating even before Nicolas Maduro was removed from power in January.
"We made the decision to stay and it put us in a position where now with things moving in a different direction we are advantaged," Wirth said, noting that Chevron produces 300,000 b/d there through its joint ventures.
With significantly improved fiscal terms under the new government of Delcy Rodriguez, Chevron intends to double production in the next five years, he said.
But he said "it will take time and money, and a lot of both, for [Venezuela] to get back up to 3 million b/d", from some 1 million b/d today. "You'll need to see more companies like ours come in," he said.
On the exploration side, Wirth said the company had done more infrastructure-led, and less frontier exploration, in the previous cycle, but pointed to increased industry interest in frontiers, driven by exploration successes in Guyana and Namibia.
"I don't think the last big basin has been found yet," Wirth said. "I think there is another one out there."

Aramco says replenishing oil stocks while meeting demand to take up to 2 years
Meeting all long-term customers' requirements
Pressure on both ends of the barrel to intensify
Oil infrastructure intact despite attacks
Saudi Arabian Oil Co. CEO Amin Nasser said Oct. 5 that replenishing oil inventories while meeting demand could take up to two years, as the world grapples with a major supply crisis as a result of the conflict in the Middle East.
Speaking at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London, Nasser said that existing buffers, including releasing inventories, have cushioned the blow to supply, but that they are finite and emergency reserves cannot fix long-term supply issues.
"Most of that came from onshore commercial inventories, which was the last major tool in the box," he said.
"If this does not normalize soon we'll find out we're building a much bigger hole in terms of inventories reaching a stress level," he said.
Aramco estimates that less than 10% of inventory is available, with most of it pipeline fill, minimum tank levels and needed for operations.

"Until it fully reopens, I'm confident that the crude reality is that pressure... will intensify," Nasser said. "While the squeeze on crude is serious, refined fuel prices have risen even more sharply," he added.
Nasser said that physical barrels cost much more than the price reflected in benchmarks.
"Sometimes $20-30/b more. The whole thing is masked by inventories," he said.
Aramco expects global demand to be resilient because the crisis is a supply disruption, not a demand disruption.
"Globally I think 2 million b/d of additional demand will come over 18-24 months, demand will be resilient going forward," he said.
Aramco expects Chinese demand to continue to increase, with demand from liquids-to-chemicals processing seen growing by 3 million b/d.

"And we have the capacity to go back up to full production and even to our current maximum sustainable capacity of 3 million b/d if we have the export routes available," he said.

"We learned from the Iraqi invasion and subsequent war in 2003 that we needed both strategic storage outside the Strait [of Hormuz] and a larger fleet that can run shuttle services outside the Gulf," he said.

Buffers `evaporating' Al-Sabah said that alternative routes and strategic storage
have acted as a buffer but cannot replace free navigation via the Strait of Hormuz.

"There's just simply not enough refining capacity in the world to make up for shuttered capacity in the Gulf," he said.

KPC is in talks with Saudi Arabia and the UAE on pipeline and port options and is looking to increase domestic storage and stocks at its overseas refineries, including in Japan and South Korea.
The company is also looking for foreign investment in projects, including pipelines and offshore fields. It has announced three offshore discoveries and Al-Sabah said that subsequent drilling has revealed further discoveries.
He called on consumers and producers alike to recognize that the situation requires a strategic response rather than a purely commercial one.
"It's a risk that we have been bearing almost entirely on our own, and it's something that needs to be internationalized or else we continue in a position -- the world continues in a position where up to 6 million barrels of refining capacity is not optimized," he said.
KPC has not amended its strategic development plans as a result of the conflict and is aiming to reach oil production capacity of 4 million b/d by 2035.

The TAEJ, also known as Guaraguao terminal, is a PDVSA crude oil storage and transportation facility located in the José Antonio Anzoátegui Industrial Complex on Venezuela 's Caribbean coast.
The TAEJ has three platforms and six loading berths capable of servicing vessels up to VLCC size. Currently, this terminal has an average operational autonomy of 1.1 days. The amount of Chevron's investment in the TAEJ was not disclosed.

The US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued updated specific and general licenses, such as Licenses 46E, 48D, and 49B, which allow companies such as Chevron, among others, to perform asset maintenance, transport, and export crude oil.
The terms of the agreement between PDVSA and Chevron to perform maintenance on the Guaraguao terminal were not available.
