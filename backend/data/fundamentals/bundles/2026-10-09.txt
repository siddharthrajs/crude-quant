Gulf of Mexico producers curtail 72% of oil output as hurricane nears: MMA
Shut-in seen at 1.459 million boe/d
BP, Chevron, Shell shut in output
A total of 1.459 million barrels/day of oil-equivalent output-- about 72% of total Gulf of Mexico production--was shut in as operators prepared for Hurricane Isaias to make landfall, the Marine Minerals Administration said Oct. 9.
Along with crude oil, nearly 59% of natural gas production, or 1.26 billion cubic feet/day, is also shut-in, the MMA said in its 2 pm ET update. The latest curtailed volumes compare with 1.283 million boe/d and 1.13 Bcf/d as of Oct. 8.
Based on data from offshore operator reports submitted at 11 am CT, personnel have been evacuated from 129 production platforms, representing 35% of the 371 manned platforms in the Gulf of Mexico, the administration said, noting personnel have been evacuated from eight non-dynamically positioned rigs, while two of the 17 dynamically positioned rigs have moved off location out of the storm's path as a precaution.
Separately, operator BP said late Oct. 8 it has temporarily curbed production and removed all personnel from its Na Kika and Thunder Horse platforms in the Gulf of Mexico as it prepares for Hurricane Isaias.
Two other operators, Chevron and Shell, announced Oct. 7 that they were proactively shutting in production and evacuating personnel from their facilities.
Chevron said it had shut production at five of its offshore facilities, while Shell said it was shutting in output at its Mars, Olympus, Ursa, Vito, and Appomattox assets.
Also, Occidental Petroleum said Oct. 8 it was closely monitoring the hurricane while implementing severe-weather preparedness plans.

Gulf states expand tanker fleets as Hormuz risks reshape export strategy
Middle Eastern countries want to own more ships
High freight rates result in short payback periods
Sovereign cover could complement private insurers
Middle Eastern countries have embarked on national tanker fleet expansions following the outbreak of the US-Iran war, which industry experts believe are aimed at enhancing their longterm export capabilities amid enduring disruptions at the Strait of Hormuz.
Washington and Tehran have been fighting over control of the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint handling 20% of global seaborne oil flows, since the conflict began at the end of February. The International Maritime Organization estimates that around 100 ships have been attacked in the region, while 24 seafarers were killed in the incidents.
With many shipowners in the private sector avoiding the region due to heightened security risks and high insurance premiums, Gulf states have been using state-owned tankers to shuttle oil to the Gulf of Oman before discharging the barrels onto their buyers' ships.
"Iran will continue to exert influence over the Strait of Hormuz across the longer term, so I expect that these shuttling networks will remain in place for years, not months," BRS Shipbrokers Head of Research Andrew Wilson told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy.
Diplomatic efforts by Iran, Oman and the US to restore normal Hormuz traffic have yielded little concrete results, with Tehran planning to charge insurance and other fees for future passage, a scheme opposed by the West and shipping industry groups.

FACTBOX: Southeast Asia accelerates efforts to shore up fuel supply, rein in energy costs
Measures include fuel-tax relief, subsidies, emergency financing programs
Countries finalize new crude-supply agreements, eye strategic stockpiles
Southeast Asian governments and energy companies are accelerating efforts to secure fuel supplies, diversify crude sources, and shield consumers from higher energy costs amid disruptions to trade flows caused by the war in the Middle East.
The measures range from fuel-tax relief, subsidies, and emergency financing programs to new crude-supply agreements, strategic stockpile studies, biofuel expansion, and maritimesecurity initiatives, highlighting the region's continued exposure to imported energy and volatile global oil markets.
The following are key facts about Southeast Asia's responses to soaring energy costs and supply disruptions:
Trade flows
Indonesia said Oct. 5 that fuel import tenders were attracting few or no bidders amid supply disruptions, forcing stateowned Pertamina to seek alternative suppliers outside its normal procurement channels. The country has lost about 25% of its oil import sources since the conflict began.
Indonesia's parliament budget committee and the government agreed Sept. 24 to cut subsidized diesel and 3-kg canister LPG volumes in the 2027 state budget, reducing the proposed energy subsidy allocation by 11.44 trillion rupiah ($639 million) as state spending on fuel support continues to rise sharply.
Indonesia has explored additional crude supplies from the US, Angola, Brazil, and Russia, and has secured commitments for Russian crude deliveries as part of broader energysecurity cooperation, its Energy and Mineral Resources Minister said Aug. 5. PetroVietnam has advanced a series of concrete initiatives with US energy companies, including Chevron, as well as financial institutions, spanning oil and gas development, energy supply, power technologies, digitalization, and investment, the Vietnamese state-owned energy group said in a statement Sept. 30.
PetroVietnam Refining and Petrochemical Corp. exchanged Sept. 23 a framework agreement with ExxonMobil Asia Pacific, securing a minimum of 2 million barrels/year for the 148,000 b/d Dung Quat refinery.
US crude imports into Vietnam have risen sharply this year. S&P Global Commodities at Sea data showed about 2.9 million barrels of US crude had been discharged into Vietnam as of Oct. 8, compared with about 1 million barrels in 2024 and no recorded US crude discharges in 2025.

Thailand's Bangchak has broadened crude sourcing for its two refineries to span North America, South America, and Australia and is selecting far-haul supplies further in advance, the company's president of refinery and marketing business group said Sept. 9. Middle Eastern crude accounted for 35% of Thailand's supply portfolio in Q2, down from 55% in Q1, according to government data.
Malaysia's Petronas said Aug. 31 that its trading arm, PETCO Trading Labuan, diversified its crude sourcing during the West Asia conflict, reducing its dependence on crude transiting the Strait of Hormuz.
Petronas's crude oil and condensate production fell 17% year over year to 657,000 boe/d in the first half of the year, while crude and condensate entitlement declined 7.6% to 427,000 boe/d, according to the company's results. The company attributed the lower total oil and gas production to planned portfolio optimization, asset transition activities, and the West Asia conflict.
Middle East Gulf crude accounted for 53% of total crude discharged into the Philippines as of Oct. 8, down from 97% in 2025, with Red Sea loadings rising to 10.3 million barrels from none the previous year.
Singapore's bunker fuel sales in September are estimated at 4.55 million metric tons, down 3.5% from August and 3.5% lower year over year, as weaker demand from crude and product tankers offset gains in container and dry bulk segments, according to the latest CAS nowcast.
Prices Vietnam has extended reduced taxes on petroleum imports,
sales and refinery feedstocks through the end of 2026 as it seeks to contain domestic prices and diversify energy supplies, according to a government resolution issued Sept. 30. From 3:00 pm local time Oct. 1, Vietnam raised the ceiling price for E5 RON 92 gasoline by 172 dong/liter ($0.0066/liter or $1.05/barrel) to 26,569 dong/liter ($1.02/liter or $162.5/b) and increased the ceiling price for E10 RON 95 gasoline by 102 dong/liter to 27,189 dong/liter.

China resumes clean oil product exports after holiday halt; Oct volumes seen lower on month
Export restrictions lifted after holiday
October exports seen at 3.5 million mt
Jet fuel accounts for 60% of exports
China is moving ahead with clean oil product exports in October after state-owned refiners resumed export activities following a brief suspension over the Oct. 1-7 National Day holiday, although volumes are expected to fall from September levels, at least four domestic refinery sources told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, Oct. 9.
"Exports were allowed to resume late yesterday after a brief halt," a refinery source in South China said Oct. 9. The source previously told Platts Oct. 8 that no new export deals were permitted to be signed on the first working day after the holiday.
"There is no longer any restriction on export destinations," the refinery source added.
Chinese refiners had largely suspended clean products exports to overseas markets ahead of and during the National Day Golden Week holiday, Platts reported earlier.

PetroChina's Guangdong Petrochemical tops 70 million mt crude throughput ahead of first turnaround
Sustains CDU utilization above 105%
First turnaround scheduled for November
China National Petroleum Corp.'s Guangdong Petrochemical has processed more than 70 million metric tons of crude since its start-up, reaching the milestone at 0400 Beijing time Sept. 18, as the refinery ran well above the nameplate capacity ahead of its first turnaround, the company said on its official WeChat account late Oct. 8.
The refinery's two crude distillation units, each of 10 million metric tons/year, have maintained operating rates above 105% for an extended period. The No. 2 CDU has been processing at 1,307 mt/hour, equivalent to a utilization rate of 109.8%, with what was previously regarded as a technical "red line" now becoming a stable operating norm, the company said.
Guangdong Petrochemical's cumulative crude throughput surpassed 50 million mt on Sept. 17, 2025, reaching 50.12 million mt since the start-up, according to the company.
The refinery processed 20.06 million mt of crude oil in 2025, reflecting sustained high utilization at the complex, Guangdong Petrochemical said.
First maintenance due Guangdong Petrochemical has been preparing for its first
turnaround after the start-up. The maintenance program is scheduled to begin in late November and last until January 2027, which could temporarily reduce crude runs and refined products output.
Guangdong Petrochemical, CNPC's largest-ever single refining and petrochemicals investment project, was fully commissioned on Feb. 27, 2023. The complex comprises 20 million mt/year of refining capacity, 2.6 million mt/year of aromatics production and 1.2 million mt/year of ethylene capacity, according to the company.
The project includes a 300,000 deadweight tons crude oil terminal and a 100,000 dwt products terminal, covering 953 hectares and 41 processing units.
Designed to process ultra-heavy and lower-quality crudes characterized by high density, sulfur, nitrogen, carbon residue, metal and acid content, the refinery has established a deepconversion processing route that maximizes value through flexible production of transportation fuels, aromatics and olefins, depending on market economics, according to the company.

OMV's oil, gas output rises in Q3 but remains close to near decade low
Crude, NGLs production increases to 176,000 boe/d
Middle East conflict cuts sales to 225,000 boe/d
Fuel sales rises 10% to 4.57 mil mt
OMV's hydrocarbon production increased slightly in the third quarter, but remained below the previous year's level due to the war in the Middle East, according to a trading update published Oct. 9.
OMV's initial quarterly estimates showed the company produced 294,000 barrels of oil equivalent/day in Q3, up slightly from 291,000 boe/d in Q2 and 288,000 boe/d in Q1, when output was at the lowest since 2013.
Crude and natural gas liquid output slightly increased from the second quarter, totaling 176,000 boe/d, up from 170,000 boe/d in Q2. Natural gas output decreased by 2,000 boe/d to 119,000boe/d.
OMV's hydrocarbon sales continued to decline, averaging 225,000 boe/d, down from 242,000 boe/d in Q2. The decrease was caused by the Middle East war and being "negatively impacted by lifting schedules in other countries," compared to the second quarter, OMV said.
The company's average realized crude oil price was $93.6/b, down from $97.8/b in Q2. In contrast, its average natural gas price rose to 49.2/MWh, up from 37.8/MWh in the previous quarter.
OMV's Middle Eastern portfolio comprises upstream working interests of 20% in the UAE's SARB and Umm Lulu oil and gas fields and a 10% stake in Iraq's Khor Mor and Chemchemal gas fields. OMV also has stakes in ADNOC Refining, ADNOC Global Trading and Borouge petrochemicals businesses in the UAE.

OMV's European indicator margin almost doubled, rising to $37.5/b from $20.33/b in Q2. Utilization across the company's facilities increased to 92%, up from 90% in Q2 and 87% in Q1. OMV operates refineries in Austria, Romania and Germany.

European-wide specification changes could lead to higher oil products output: UNEM
Fuel spec changes need to be technically and economically viable
Jet fuel deficit persists in Italy despite 30% rise in production
Changes to fuel technical specifications can lead to further increases in the output of oil products, provided such changes are technically and environmentally viable and approved on a European level, Gianni Murano, the President of Italy's UNEM (Unione Energie per la Mobilita), said in a statement Oct.8.
Murano's comments follow a call from European leaders in late September for joint policy action, including relaxed fuel quality standards and revised biofuels regulations, to help bring fuel costs down from record highs.
Several countries have already introduced emergency measures to try and limit price hikes, while France authorized the domestic sale of summer-specification diesel until Nov. 15, two weeks later than its usual Nov. 1 deadline, citing supply difficulties due to the Middle East crisis.
Unem (Unione Energie per la Mobilita), the association that represents the sector's leading companies, also highlighted in

the statement that Italy's refining sector is "willing to assess the scope for further production increases, via a plant-by-plant evaluation."
While Italy is currently among the European countries that has sufficient refining capacity to meet domestic demand for fuels such as petrol and diesel, a significant production deficit remains for jet fuel, despite a roughly 30% increase in production in 2026, the Energy Security and Industry Ministries also said in a separate statement.
Italy's refining capacity is at about 83.3 million tonnes per year. In 2026, the average utilization rate was estimated by Unem at around 80%, with a peak of 86% recorded in August.

Nigeria to cap retail fuel prices to tackle rising costs
Government negotiating price cap for retail gasoline
NNPC offers 30-day discount at service stations
Opposition parties call for return to fuel subsidy
The Nigerian government is taking measures to cap gasoline prices that retailers can charge at the country's fuel stations in a bid to combat soaring global prices linked to the Middle East conflict, its finance minister announced in a press briefing on Oct. 8.
Responding to ongoing volatility putting pressure on the Nigerian consumer, the government is negotiating a new price ceiling with marketers and retailers, which would be set at 1,350 nairas/ liter ($1.02/l), Finance Minister Taiwo Oyedele told reporters in Abuja.
"Pump prices do not have to follow every swing in global crude prices or the exchange rate," the minister said, adding that refiners and importers should absorb any difference between the price cap and prevailing market rates.
In addition to the proposed cap, retail stations managed by the Nigerian National Petroleum Corp. are additionally offering a 30-day discount on gasoline supplies, with priority for public transport operators, the minister said.
He separated the proposed interventions from the fuel subsidies historically offered by the Nigerian government, which were abolished as part of sweeping economic reforms introduced by President Bola Tinubu when he took office in 2023.
"This is neither a subsidy nor a price control; it is designed to smooth prices over time, rather than suppressing them," Oyedele said. "Returning to subsidy will make matters worse, not better," he said.
In 2022, when the outbreak of the Russia-Ukraine war sent oil prices soaring, the scheme cost Nigeria roughly $10 billion, the International Monetary Fund reported, warning that the policy needed to be scrapped to manage the country's debt.
In the energy sector, subsidies also contributed to smuggling across Nigeria's borders, inflating the country's import volumes at a time when it relied almost exclusively on fuel produced abroad.

UAE, Saudi Arabia sign MOU for rail and energy connectivity: WAM
Feasibility studies planned for cooperation
Deal comes as Hormuz crisis impacts ports and oil exports
The UAE and Saudi Arabia have signed a memorandum of understanding to work more closely on rail and energy connectivity, UAE state-run news agency WAM reported Oct. 8.
The countries, which have had some of their ports and oil exports choked off by the crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, will work to develop rail connectivity between the countries and create infrastructure to improve energy-sector integration as part of the agreement, WAM said. State interests Firms controlled by Bahri, ADNOC Logistics & Services, Asyad
Shipping, and Kuwait Petroleum Corp. own 89 crude tankers with a carrying capacity of 25.8 million deadweight tons and 106 chemical/product tankers with 6.71 million dwt on the waters, according to company reports and S&P Global Energy Horizons data.
In early August, ADNOC L&S, the listed shipping arm of the UAE's Abu Dhabi National Oil Co., announced its purchases of six VLCCs and five very large gas carriers. KPC has been seeking to expand its own tanker fleet to shuttle services, according to company executives.
Asyad has an order book of 14 tankers built before the current crisis, though the Omani state-backed company said it has been avoiding Hormuz passage for safety reasons. Iraqi Oil Tankers Co. transported crude out of Hormuz with a VLCC under its operation for the first time in early October, and the company plans to purchase and own crude tankers, according to the state-run Iraqi News Agency.
Fotios Katsoulas, tanker research director at S&P Global Energy Horizons, said fleet expansion would allow Middle Eastern oil producers to maintain exports during periods when ship availability tightens and freight rates spike.
"What we are seeing is primarily a long-term strategic shift rather than a temporary reaction to the current conflict," Katsoulas said. "Owning tanker capacity is increasingly being viewed as part of a broader energy security strategy."
Economics Increased STS transfers have led to higher tanker
requirements while risk-averse ship operators are steering away from the region, tightening tonnage supply and prompting freight rates to keep breaking records in the Middle East and beyond in recent weeks.
Platts assessed the VLCC rate for transporting 265,000 metric tons of crude from the Persian Gulf to West Coast India, another emerging STS hub, at $98.01/mt on Oct. 8, equivalent to a lump-

sum cost of nearly $26 million. This was 412% higher than the pre-conflict level on Feb. 27.
A roundtrip voyage for this trade could take less than two weeks. In contrast, the secondhand price for a 15-year-old secondhand VLCC is $160 million despite rising 177% year on year, according to Xclusiv Shipbrokers' estimate. Based on prevailing spot freight rates and excluding operating, financing and insurance costs, the figures suggest a payback period of roughly three months.
"It is ... relatively easy for a producer to amortize the price of a VLCC," said Wilson, adding that buying tankers would allow the producer to keep assets for a longer term compared with periodchartering ships.
Trade-offs Aside from free-on-board sales from their home ports, Middle
Eastern producers traditionally relied more on chartering ships for some months or years rather than buying tankers outright when in need to deliver oil to their customers.
But shipowners in time charters could refuse to enter war zones, and Andrew Rigden Green, a Hong Kong-based partner of law firm WFW, said charterers could face higher payments if there are delays -- and scheduling tends to be more difficult during conflicts as seafarers spend time finding safest transit means.
"From an operational perspective, ownership mainly provides security of capacity, faster reaction time, greater control over vessel positioning and less dependence on charter-market availability," said Eirini Diamantara, an Xclusiv researcher.
"The trade-off, of course, is that the owner assumes the capital cost, operating cost and asset-value risk."
In the event of a total loss of a laden VLCC, Middle Eastern producers could face losses in the range of hundreds of million dollars on top of potential cleaning costs for oil spills without insurance coverage. But cargo insurance premiums for Hormuz transits have increased by at least 30 times during the conflict, and hull premiums by 40 times, according to insurance industry participants.
Saudi and Emirati state-backed firms have been expanding maritime insurance offerings, which could lead to more coverage availability. That would complement commercial insurers rather than squeeze them out, according to some brokers.
While there have been government offerings on hull and cargo, it would be challenging for sovereign funds to cover third-party liabilities like pollution due to the difficulties in limiting damage, according to Marcus Baker, insurance brokerage Marsh's global head for marine.
Alec Russell, managing director of marine cargo at brokerage Gallagher, separately said cargo insurance is "incredibly cost effective" as a "risk-transfer mechanism".
"Even at a massive sovereign level, there's still a big requirement," Russel added. "I don't think that will change in the long run."

State-run YPF has a 55% share of diesel and gasoline sales, trailed by Raizen, BP-backed Pan American Energy and Trafigura.
Argentina has about 560,000 b/d of installed refining capacity, but demand generally runs at 550,000 b/d.
