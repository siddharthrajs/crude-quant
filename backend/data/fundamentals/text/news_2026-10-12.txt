Spot premiums for Cold Lake and Access Western Blend crude oil delivered to East Asia via the Pacific have been trending higher as major regional refiners place greater value on the Canadian heavy sour grades amid disruptions to heavy sour Middle Eastern supplies, according to North Asian refinery sources and South Korean foreign affairs and trade ministries.
Although refiners have secured ample non-Middle Eastern crude since April as part of supply-diversification efforts, alternative sources of heavy sour crude remain limited. As a result, Canadian Cold Lake and Access Western Blend grades are gaining popularity among South Korean, Japanese and Chinese refiners, according to feedstock and trading managers based in Ulsan, Chiba, Beijing, Yeosu and Negishi who spoke with Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, during market discussions over Oct. 6-12.
Platts assessed Cold Lake at a record-high premium of $9.5/b against front-month Dubai on CFR South Korea basis Sept. 21. The premium averaged $4.33/b to date in October, compared to September's average premium of $3.67/b and the first half's average of a 5-cent/b discount.
In China's refining sector, Canadian heavy sour crude cargoes were most recently offered at premiums of up to $14/b to ICE Brent futures, trading managers and feedstock sources at several Shandong-based refiners told Platts Oct. 12.

BP looking to grow upstream presence in India amid new incentives: CEO
Says India's 'future potential' will reshape BP's global portfolio
Energy markets proved more adaptable than expected
China's resilience reflects decades of hard work to diversify
BP is looking to expand its presence in India's upstream oil and gas sector, encouraged by the country's untapped exploration potential, attractive fiscal and legal terms and government policies aimed at attracting foreign investment, The Economic Times quoted CEO Meg O'Neill as saying Oct. 10.
"We always ask ourselves where we have exploration opportunities coupled with attractive fiscal and legal terms, and a government that is supportive of foreign investment coming in. Well, with India, we have all of those elements," O'Neill told the Indian news organization during her visit to India last week.
O'Neill said the introduction of policies such as Samudra Manthan, or the National Offshore Exploration Scheme, is intended to encourage companies to undertake more exploration and support the infrastructure required to bring discovered resources into production.
"And so, we are quite excited about exploring," she said. The federal Cabinet in late July approved investments of up to 840 billion Indian rupees ($8.8 billion) over five years to advance offshore oil and gas exploration under the scheme. The initiative is expected to catalyze reserve accretion of more than 600 million metric tons of oil equivalent by fiscal year 2030-31 (AprilMarch), the petroleum ministry said July 31. The government will fund up to 50% of eligible deep-water exploration drilling costs, capped at $71 million per well. By partially covering the costs, the scheme could significantly improve the economics of frontier exploration. The allocation comes as India has offered 18 deep-water and ultra-deepwater blocks under the 10th and 11th Open Acreage Licensing Policy rounds, or OALP-X and OALP-XI, analysts and officials said Oct 6. India's potential is also relevant to BP's broader effort to simplify and reshape its global portfolio. The company is assessing its businesses based on profitability and long-term growth prospects, and India stands out on both measures, O'Neill said. "When we look at the portfolio, we ask ourselves, what are the assets that are profitable? What are the assets that have future growth potential?" she said. "When we look at the India business, we see that future potential." She added that the company is actively evaluating the current exploration rounds in India.
Interconnected economies O'Neill said that over the past four decades, global trade had
created highly interconnected economies, allowing goods and services to move efficiently and reach consumers at relatively low cost.

Although ongoing conflicts and growing divisions in the international order have prompted governments to strengthen resilience and reduce dependence in certain areas, many supply chains and trade flows are so deeply embedded that major changes will be difficult to achieve, she added.
"If you think about some of the tariffs imposed recently, they have not particularly moved the needle on global trade. So, I think the world will continue to be quite adaptable, but it is going to be volatile," O'Neill said.
Planning is particularly challenging in an uncertain environment, although volatility has long been an inherent feature of the oil and gas industry, she added.
"If you look back over time, we have been an industry that has periodically had quite significant price shocks and price spikes," O'Neill said. "You always need to be able to see through them and get to the underlying big macro supply-demand questions. Policy changes are a risk everywhere we operate in the world."
The closure of the Strait of Hormuz was unexpected, but markets demonstrated a greater ability to adjust than anticipated. This resilience was partly supported by Russian and Iranian cargoes on water, as well as available petroleum reserves, which provided a temporary buffer. However, much of that spare capacity has now been used, she added.
A prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz would make market planning extremely difficult. While oil prices could rise to about $150 a barrel if the disruption continued and shipments through the strait stopped completely, prices could fall to about $50 a barrel if the conflict eased, she added.
"That would be a scenario where peace is declared on multiple fronts, Russia's unsanctioned, Iran's unsanctioned and Venezuela continues to ramp up production. So, it makes it incredibly hard to plan," O'Neill said. "Now, in our business, we try to plan for the worst and be prepared for a situation where we are able to respond more dynamically to meet our customers' needs."
Upstream fallout BP has some upstream operations in the Gulf, including
production in Iraq and production entitlements in Abu Dhabi, but the overall impact on its output is modest. Given the current security risks, the company is not deploying BP-chartered vessels to the region, as the level of risk is too high, O'Neill said.
China's ability to reduce its oil imports during the turbulent period helped ease pressure on the market, she added.
"What we have seen in the past six months is the result of 20 plus years of hard work to diversify its energy system, to build domestic energy resources, whatever is available. It is also a nation that plans for long-term energy resilience," O'Neill said.
"Japan and [South] Korea are also nations with robust economies that do not have the benefit of significant domestic energy, and they are very thoughtful and very long term in their planning, but they have not had the ability to absorb the shock the way China has," she added.
-- Sambit Mohanty

Eni to sell stakes in East Kalimantan oil and gas blocks to Pertamina, MedcoEnergi
Companies to acquire stakes in seven blocks
North Hub targets 1 Bscf/d of gas in Q4 2028
Geliga holds estimated 5 Tcf of gas in place
Italy's Eni has signed two binding agreements to sell 10% interests in seven East Kalimantan oil and gas blocks to Indonesia's Pertamina and Medco Energi Internasional, bringing the companies into the upstream assets linked to the Bontang LNG plant, the companies said Oct. 12.
Pertamina's upstream subsidiary, PT Pertamina Hulu Energi, signed a sale and purchase agreement in October to acquire interests in the North Ganal, Rapak and East Sepinggan blocks. The deal would give PHE a 10% stake in both North Ganal and Rapak and raise its interest in East Sepinggan to 25% from 15%, according to company statements.
MedcoEnergi agreed to acquire a 10% non-operating participating interest in each of the Muara Bakau, Ganal, West Ganal and East Ganal production-sharing contracts. The portfolio includes producing fields, development projects and the Geliga gas discovery, the company said.
Completion of both transactions is subject to government and regulatory approvals, Eni said. The transaction values were not disclosed.
The assets are operated by Searah, a 50-50 joint venture established by Eni and Malaysia's Petronas in June to combine their businesses in Indonesia and Malaysia.
The deals bring the Indonesian companies into a portfolio being developed to increase domestic gas supply and LNG exports, with the North Hub and South Hub projects scheduled to begin production in 2028.
MedcoEnergi CEO Ronald Gunawan said the acquisition would broaden the company's LNG exposure and strengthen its production base while adding exploration opportunities in the Kutei Basin.
The deal "expands our presence into an established LNG value chain, supported by world-class infrastructure and operations," Gunawan said in the statement.
PHE said its interests in North Ganal and Rapak would support the development of the Kutei North Hub and create synergies with Pertamina's East Kalimantan operations, including PT Badak NGL, while helping optimize existing gas, LPG and LNG infrastructure.
The $11.8 billion North Hub project, comprising the Geng North and Gehem fields, targets gas output of 1 billion standard cubic feet/day and condensate production of 80,000 barrels/ day in the fourth quarter of 2028, Indonesia's Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources said July 23. Construction of the project's floating production, storage and offloading vessel topsides began that month.
Eni took final investment decisions on the North Hub and

South Hub projects on March 18. South Hub, covering the Gendalo and Gandang fields, will connect subsea wells to the existing Jangkrik floating production unit, while North Hub will use a new FPSO.
The two hubs are expected to reach combined plateau production of 2 Bscf/d of gas and 90,000 b/d of condensate in 2029, Eni said. The gas will supply the domestic pipeline network and Bontang LNG, with the development plan including the reactivation of idle Train F. Condensate will be processed and stored offshore for tanker export.
MedcoEnergi's acquisition also includes exposure to the Geliga discovery in the Ganal PSC. Eni said May 7 that well testing supported preliminary estimates of about 5 trillion cubic feet of gas and 300 million barrels of condensate in place.
Together with the nearby undeveloped Gula discovery, estimated to hold 2 Tcf of gas and 75 million barrels of condensate in place, Geliga could support additional output of about 1 Bscf/d of gas and 80,000 b/d of condensate, according to Eni's early evaluations.
Eni said in May that it was preparing a development plan for a third production hub and studying the potential reactivation of up to two more idle trains at Bontang LNG.
MedcoEnergi's oil and gas production totaled 170,000 barrels of oil equivalent/day in the first half of 2026, with gas accounting for about 72% of the total, the company said in its results statement Sept. 21.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, assessed JKM, the benchmark price reflecting LNG delivered to Northeast Asia, for November at $26.242/million British thermal units on Oct. 9, down 45.5 cents/MMBtu day over day. The JKM December derivatives were at $26.450/MMBtu.

Gabon in talks with more IOCs on offshore exploration after sector rejig: official
New competitive hydrocarbon code weeks away
Production stagnates amid limited exploration
BEAC row effectively solved amid direct talks with firms
Gabon is holding talks with a handful of oil majors about following BP, Shell and ExxonMobil into its deep-water offshore, after learning from past mistakes and reforming its regulatory framework, a senior energy ministry official told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, on Oct. 12.
The Central African country, an OPEC member with a 100-year history of oil exploration, has had its crude production flatline in recent years amid underinvestment in exploration. The country pumped 230,000 b/d in September, according to the Platts OPEC Survey, around the same level as in 2016.
Speaking on the sidelines of the Africa Energy Week conference in Cape Town, Freedy Nzue Nzue, senior advisor at the petroleum ministry, said Gabon's government "understood the weaknesses we had in the past," particularly when it came to incentivizing new offshore drilling, and was now holding talks with "three or four" oil companies about a potential entry.
It follows deals with ExxonMobil last year and with BP and Shell this year, which it hopes will lead to production-sharing contracts.
Nzue said Gabon had rejigged its fiscal regime to incentivize ultra-deepwater exploration, in which a single well can cost as much as $200 million. The country is also weeks away from unveiling a new hydrocarbon code, which will be among the most competitive in Africa, he said.

Saudi Aramco CEO says global commercial oil stocks `barely a cushion at all'
Diesel close to $200/b: Nasser at WPC
Refilling inventories could take two years
Hormuz disruptions threaten energy security
Global commercial onshore oil inventories at less than 6 billion barrels are "barely a cushion at all" against the unforeseen, and "if that is not scary enough, the vast majority of these barrels are locked up," Saudi Aramco CEO Amin Nasser told the WPC Energy Congress in Riyadh Oct. 12.
Inventories "can never be a permanent solution to energy security," Nasser said. "Globally, the squeeze on crude oil is serious. Making the challenge even worse is the spike in refined fuel prices," he said, adding that diesel is around $200/barrel. Refilling inventories while meeting demand could take up to two years, Nasser said during the speech, according to a statement on Aramco's website.
Shipping disruptions and damaged pipelines and plants due to the Iran war since the end of February have upended norms for producers and consumers in the Middle East and beyond.
"Until Hormuz fully reopens and confidence returns, the pressure across the energy chain will only intensify," Nasser said.
Saudi Arabia itself cannot safeguard its energy and shipping deliveries on its own and must involve all nations to protect its infrastructure and routes, Nasser said. Disruption to trade in Hormuz and for other energy infrastructure has "tested every link" in the chain from energy production to consumption, he said.
"When energy infrastructure in this region is targeted, the consequences reach economies everywhere," Nasser said. "The kingdom has invested deeply in protecting these supplies. But no nation, even one as capable and confident as ours, can safeguard them alone."
Master Gas Saudi Aramco's Master Gas System has operated "reliably"
through the Iran war, connecting energy resources with electricity generation, desalination, refining, petrochemicals, and industry, Nasser said. Saudi Arabia is targeting roughly an 80% increase in sales/gas production capacity by 2030 versus 2021 production levels, according to Nasser.
"We are also exploring future opportunities in hydrogen" alongside carbon capture, utilization, and storage and "circular carbon technologies," Nasser said.
The country is also building solar and wind power capacity to diversify electricity supplies and help to lower emissions, he said. Gas and renewables will provide the country's electricity in equal shares by 2030, according to the statement.
The company's refineries are also providing gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, asphalt, and industrial feedstocks to the domestic economy, while petrochemicals are supporting Saudi Arabia's manufacturing, he said.

Saudi Arabia signs energy cooperation deals with Kuwait, Qatar, Egypt
Deals focus on oil, gas, power
Egypt crude pipeline flows hit 1.25 million b/d
Kuwait seeks power grid cooperation aid
Saudi Arabia signed wide-ranging memorandums of understanding with Kuwait, Qatar and Egypt to bolster cooperation on energy and electricity, among other sectors, according to multiple press releases from Oct. 11.
The ongoing Strait of Hormuz crisis has triggered crossborder talks and new initiatives among Gulf Cooperation Council states and Middle Eastern countries as they work to mitigate the effects of shipping disruptions on their economies and energy trade.
With Egypt, the countries will look to more closely cooperate "in the fields of trade, exchange, transportation, storage, and distribution of crude oil and petroleum products and natural gas, in addition to developing the infrastructure and logistics services associated with these activities," a statement from Egypt's petroleum ministry said.
Opportunities for joint cooperation on petrochemical projects will also be studied, the statement said.

Egypt has become an increasingly important outlet for Saudi Arabian crude via the SUMED pipeline since the onset of the war at the end of February disrupted crude exports via Ras Tanura. In September, Saudi Arabian crude flows through the Egyptian SUMED pipeline, which feeds into the Sidi Kerir terminal, rose to 1.25 million b/d, according to Hafnia estimates, reaching their highest level since April 2020 after Houthi attacks escalated in late July.
The Qatar-Saudi MOU covers cooperation in sectors like oil and gas and their derivatives, as well as refining, petrochemicals, electricity, clean hydrogen and its derivatives, renewable energy, and energy efficiency and storage, a QatarEnergy statement said.
With Kuwait, Saudi Arabia will look to strengthen ties on electricity interconnection and exports, energy storage, among other areas, according to a statement carried by Kuwait's state news KUNA.
Kuwait grapples with regular power shortages in the summers and relies heavily on direct burn for generation, CERA said in an August report.
Some work has been done on intra-GCC electricity sharing through the GCC Interconnection Authority, which was established in 2001, CERA said in an October 2025 report. By 2011, the six GCC members were connected, but the grid was primarily used for emergency and reserve sharing only and crossborder flows were low, CERA said. More power has been traded for economic purposes, reaching 1.3 terawatt-hours in 2023, according to CERA.

Russian diesel imports unlikely to ease US fuel prices
Russia lacks capacity to boost diesel exports
Proposed volumes match recent export levels
Legal barriers may block Russian fuel US imports
US authorization to import Russian diesel is unlikely to have a material impact on diesel prices in the near term because Russia has little capacity to boost exports, and the US license could also face legal hurdles, energy analysts say.
"The amount of diesel Russia can provide would be a drop in the bucket compared to global demand," said Masha Kotkin, a fellow at the Center for Strategic and International Studies. "If you are asking about US diesel prices, I don't think it's going to make

a difference. It is not very much in the scheme of things." US President Donald Trump said on Truth Social Oct. 9 that
"Russia will immediately supply over 300,000 Tons of Diesel Fuel to the American and Global Marketplace, another 500,000 Tons during the month of November, and 1,000,000 Tons immediately thereafter."
The US Treasury Department also issued a sanctions waiver allowing the sale, delivery, and importation of diesel fuel, including importation into the US, until April 7, 2027.
Russia has modest excess volumes to send to the global market, Rachel Ziemba, a senior adviser with Horizon Engage, told Platts, a part of S&P Global Energy, in an email.

"Of greater impact would be if a lasting energy ceasefire were to be enacted, allowing Russia to repair its refineries and increase production/exports - but that would take many months," the analysts said. Roughly half of Russia's refinery capacity is offline after repeated Ukrainian attacks, the analysts said.

importing Russian diesel to the US. A bipartisan group of six senators led by Senator Jeanne Shaheen (Democrat-New Hampshire), on Oct. 11 issued a statement arguing that Congress explicitly banned Russian energy imports in 2022.
"President Trump's decision to allow Russian diesel imports to the US runs directly counter to that law. The Administration has also failed to consult with Congress or submit any explanation to justify its decision as required by statute," the statement said.
Sanctions on Russia are not like those on Venezuela and elsewhere that can be suspended by the executive branch as desired, said Ellen Wald, president of Transversal Consulting.

Russia partially lifts diesel export ban
Exports of 500,000 mt/month approved
Analysts see limited market impact
The Russian government has partially lifted a diesel export ban, after US President Donald Trump said that Russia had agreed to supply global markets amid concerns over supply shortages and high prices.
Analysts said that Russian refiners are able to supply some volumes to global markets, but are unlikely to cover the supply deficit caused by interruptions to exports from the Middle East.
The Russian government said that the export ban was partially lifted from Oct. 10 and at this stage allows for the export of 500,000 mt of diesel.
"Russian oil companies, in coordination with the government, will immediately begin contracting diesel supplies with foreign counterparties," a government statement said.
Russian Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said that Russia is ready to supply 300,000 mt of diesel to the US market and other partners in October, according to the Tass news agency.
He confirmed Trump's estimates that Russia will supply up to 500,000 mt in November and said that supplies could rise to 1 million mt in December, the report said.
"Going forward, the volume could rise to 3 million mt/month. At the same time, the Russian market will also be fully supplied with diesel," Novak said.
Independent energy analyst George Voloshin said that supplying up to 500,000 mt/month is operationally feasible using existing inventories and seasonal refinery turnarounds, but scaling up to 1-3 million mt/month would face severe physical bottlenecks.
"Sustained Ukrainian drone strikes on secondary refining and hydrocracking units across western Russia have constrained domestic production of high-grade winter diesel. Forcing multimillion-ton monthly exports without fully repaired processing units risks creating domestic supply crunches and sharp wholesale price spikes inside Russia," he said.
S&P Global Energy CERA analysts said that they see the market impact of the deal as limited, in a note released Oct. 11.
"[Global] diesel exports are down by over 1 million b/d largely due to refinery outages in Russia and the Middle East. These Russian volumes, if truly incremental, would be only 10% of that global total," they said.
They see a lasting energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine as more consequential, if it comes into force.
Domestic supplies Diesel supply has recently been sufficient to cover both retail
and spot market demand inside Russia, according to Russiabased market sources. Market participants said that in contrast

to gasoline, diesel is available at all retail stations. The farming sector has also been provided with the necessary volumes during the autumn agricultural campaign, which is now drawing to a close.
Market participants had said previously that the diesel ban would need to be lifted as refineries would otherwise struggle to place the summer diesel production, which is no longer needed domestically as the country switches to intermediate and winter grades.
Russia imposed a diesel export ban on so-called nonproducers, which includes traders and small refineries with up to 1 million mt/year processing capacity, at the end of January 2026. This ban was set to be in force until the end of January 2027. On July 9, the diesel export ban was also applied to large refineries and was planned to be in effect until the end of October.
Voloshin said that the deal is unlikely to impact Russian crude output, which has fallen in recent months, partially due to strikes on refineries.
"Russian refiners may run primary distillation units harder at the expense of product quality and yield, or, if domestic downstream capacity cannot absorb more runs, Russia will simply continue exporting unrefined crude east to Asian buyers while keeping overall wellhead production steady," he said.
