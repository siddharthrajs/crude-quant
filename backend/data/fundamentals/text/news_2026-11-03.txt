Saudi Aramco working on East-West pipeline expansion, other export routes: CEO
Aramco advances engineering work on expansion
East-West pipe brought online days after attack
Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline is undergoing preliminary engineering work for an expansion beyond its 7 million barrels/ day capacity, while more export routes are also being developed, Saudi Arabian Oil Co. CEO Amin Nasser told analysts in a Nov. 3 earnings call.
"Preliminary engineering is underway for an expansion of the East-West pipeline," Nasser said, adding that, in line with energy minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman's Oct. 12 announcement of upcoming additional pipeline capacity, the company is working toward "major expansions [to Aramco's] capacity going much more than the 7 million barrels/day that we currently have."
Bin Salman had said at the WPC Energy Congress in Riyadh Oct. 12 that the kingdom planned to expand the East-West pipeline's capacity to up to 10 million b/d within two to three years, alongside plans to temporarily convert a gas pipeline to provide an estimated 1.2 million b/d of crude transport capacity within seven to eight weeks from then.
"We are also in the process of -- we're doing the engineering, actually -- other routes in addition to the current routes," Nasser said on Aramco's third-quarter earnings call Nov. 3.
Nasser declined to comment further on the pipeline expansion plans during the call and said projects are still in progress.

Tanker fleet growth threatens freight boom, bullish secondhand markets: analysts
Fleet capacity surges 23% through 2029
Supply growth risks eroding record rates
Market uncertainty amid geopolitical conflicts
A wave of tanker deliveries is set to test whether geopolitical disruption and longer trading routes can continue supporting charter rates and ship values, according to S&P Global Energy CERA's latest fleet outlook, after an extraordinary freight market rally pushed some secondhand ship values above newbuilding order prices.
Taking newbuild order books and scrapping into account, CERA analysts forecast global tanker capacity will rise from 703.72 million deadweight tons at the end of 2025 to 743.56 million dwt in 2026, 778.35 million dwt in 2027 and 830.61 million dwt in 2028, before reaching 862.98 million dwt in 2029. That would represent cumulative growth of 22.6% over four years.

India's crude imports hit record high in Oct as refiners stock up for festivals, product exports
Imports hit 5.54 million b/d, surpass March 2025 levels
Freight costs pressure Russian crude economics
Refiners maximize runs as crude supplies recover
Crude imports by Indian refiners rose to an all-time high of 5.54 million b/d in October, as refiners capitalized on improved availability to build supplies ahead of festival demand, while processing higher volumes to produce additional middle distillates for international markets facing supply tightness, industry sources and analysts said Nov. 3.
Domestic-bound crude imports in October were up 9% from both September and the same month a year earlier, according to data from S&P Global Commodities at Sea. The monthly average surpassed the previous high of 5.50 million b/d imported in March 2025.
"Russian imports have come off the peaks we saw in June and July, but they're still close to 2 million b/d in October. At the same time, we are seeing more barrels coming out of the Middle East, particularly from Saudi Arabia and Iraq, and Indian refiners are taking advantage of that availability ahead of stronger seasonal demand," said Premasish Das, executive director for oil analytics at S&P Global Energy CERA.
Russian crude shipments to India rose sharply in the first half of 2026, increasing from 1.17 million barrels/day in January to a peak of 2.43 million b/d in June, according to CAS. Flows eased to 2.05 million b/d in July, 1.98 million b/d in August, and 1.64 million b/d in September, but picked up to nearly 2.1 million b/d in October.
"An Oct. 16 US waiver reduced the immediate risk of additional measures affecting buyers of Russian crude. However, the waiver has no specified expiry and remains revocable, while existing Russia-related sanctions continue to leave importers exposed to renewed policy pressure," said Saransh Katiyar, liquid bulk analyst at CAS.
US President Donald Trump signed an executive order Oct. 16, waiving measures included in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act for the largest buyers of Russian oil, citing "national interests," days before they were due to take effect.
The legislation targeted buyers of Russian hydrocarbons, allowing the president to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported from the five largest buyers of Russian crude oil or natural gas by total volume over the year before enactment, among other measures.
Countries such as India can continue to import Russian crude without immediately facing additional US penalties or tariffs. However, the waiver does not permanently revoke the law, and it can be withdrawn. As a result, buyers may choose to diversify their oil supplies and remain cautious about future sanctions risks, refining sources said.

Freight economics CERA's Das added that the economics of Russian barrels
were changing as freight costs remain high. Uncertainty around US concessions on Russian oil, the impact of drone attacks on Russian refining, and competition from Chinese buyers also mean Indian refiners will continue to look for opportunities across different sources of supply, depending on availability and relative attractiveness.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, last assessed Dirty Black Sea to West Coast India freight at a lumpsum of $28 million on Oct. 30, nearly double the year-to-date average of $14.5 million.
Analysts said the increase in October crude imports for Indian refiners was also supported by higher arrivals from Angola and the US.
"Favorable transatlantic arbitrage economics encouraged additional US crude flows into India, while stronger liftings of Angolan heavy-sweet and medium-sour grades also contributed to the rise," Katiyar said.
US crude flows in 2026 averaged 125,000 b/d over JanuaryOctober, with inflows reaching 150,000 b/d in October, up 16% from September levels, CAS data showed.
In addition, Middle Eastern producers were making a clear effort to regain market share, giving Indian refiners a wider range of supply options and contributing to the increase in overall imports, Das said, adding that India's crude imports were likely to remain elevated, supported by strong refining margins and the start-up of new capacity.
Improving availability "Indian refiners are swiftly capitalizing on improved crude
availability to maximize utilization rates and secure more cargoes to meet both festive demand at home and export more volumes to deficit markets," said B. Anand, former CEO of Nayara Energy and founder of consultancy firm Meridian Partners.
Indian refineries processed 5.62 million b/d in September, the oil ministry's provisional estimates showed, reflecting 9.1% higher processing from a year earlier as imports by the world's thirdlargest buyer rose despite supply concerns due to the Middle East conflict.

Ukraine did not attack Russian refineries from Oct. 16-29, which marked the longest strike-free period for Russia's refining system in more than seven months.
Ukraine resumed its attacks on the refineries on Oct. 30, with damage reported at four refineries since then. The renewed attacks on Russian refineries "could lead to a reduction in supply volumes," according to Russia's Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak.

Apache's North Sea oil workers set to intensify strike over pay
170 workers to down tools three times in Nov
Will affect US firm's Forties, Beryl oil fields
Unite union also flags strike on Harbour platforms
North Sea oil workers with US firm Apache are set to intensify their ongoing strike, with three 24-hour periods of industrial action in the coming days, labor union Unite said Nov. 3, as a weeks-long dispute over pay and conditions remains unresolved.
In a statement, Unite said 170 Apache offshore workers will go on strike on Nov. 4, Nov. 13 and Nov. 15, impacting the Forties

Alpha, Forties Bravo, Forties Delta, Forties Charlie, Forties Echo, Beryl Bravo and Beryl Alpha platforms.
The new threats follow 24 hours of strike action in late October, which brought all seven assets "to a standstill", Unite said. Involved are electrical, instrument, mechanical and production technicians, as well as telecoms technicians and radio operators.
The union previously warned that the action could "severely disrupt" the UK's fuel supply and cause the Charlie platform to shut down, which in turn could even close the Forties Pipeline System, the country's biggest crude conduit, because of a lack of pressure.
Some 80 offshore fields flow through pipelines into the FPS, which transports 200,000-250,000 b/d of crude, or 30-40% of the UK's output.
Apache told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, in October: "Based on our current contingency plans, we do not anticipate that industrial action would affect other producers' ability to continue operating through the Forties Pipeline System."
The company said it would maintain experienced personnel on Forties Charlie to support third-party production, even if all five of its Forties platforms were suspended.
Ineos FPS, the operator of the key pipeline, did not respond to a request for comment.
The industrial action comes after Apache's workers rejected a pay offer from the company, according to Unite.
"Many of our Apache members are being offered a real terms pay cut," said Dougie Maguire, Unite's deputy Scottish secretary, in the union's statement.
"The previous stoppage brought all seven assets to a standstill, and we have no choice but to escalate our action until Apache gets back round the table with an offer which is acceptable to our members."
In a statement to Platts on Nov. 3, Apache acknowledged "Unite's announcement of further industrial action."
"Not all employees in the bargaining unit participated in the Oct. 21 strike. However, Apache took the operational decision to shut down its North Sea offshore assets for the 24-hour action to ensure the safety of personnel on board. Shutdown and restart were completed safely and without incident, and third-party producers continued operating through the Forties Pipeline System as normal," the spokesperson said.
"During any further industrial action, we will assess staffing and competence on each platform and continue operations where it is safe to do so. Where sufficient cover is unavailable, we will shut down in a controlled manner."
The company added that its final offer to workers is "fair, competitive within the industry and recognizes the contribution of our offshore workforce." It said it had engaged "constructively," including by inviting mediators to help find a resolution, and remains "open to dialogue."
Apache North Sea Production Ltd. is part of the US company APA Corp. It entered the North Sea in 2003 with a 97% interest in the Forties field, which produced over 500,000 b/d at its peak in the late 1970s.

Pipeline shipped 7.573 mil mt in Jan-Sept
Kazakhstan has resumed crude flows through the AtyrauSamara pipeline following a one-day halt, the Kazakh energy ministry told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, Nov. 3.
"As of Nov. 3, 2026, oil transportation via the Atyrau-Samara pipeline is proceeding as normal. Pumping operations were resumed following a technical inspection," the ministry said in a statement.
The Samara production and dispatch hub in Russia came under a Ukrainian drone attack Nov. 2, after which oil transportation through the Atyrau-Samara pipeline was suspended, according to the ministry.
State pipeline operator KazTransOil, which operates the Kazakh section of the Atyrau-Samara line, did not respond to a Platts request for comment.

Kenyan panel dismisses challenge to Dangote's $16B Lamu refinery
Consumer group's petition rejected by PPP panel
Refinery could process 700,000 b/d from 2030
Regional governments considering equity stakes
Kenya's Dangote refinery has seen off a legal challenge to its $16 billion oil refinery project in Lamu from a Kenyan consumer advocacy organization, weeks after it broke ground on the East African plant, Kenya's treasury cabinet secretary John Mbadi said Nov. 3.
The case was filed by the Consumers Federation of Kenya, or COFEK, which demanded disclosure of the government's financial commitments regarding the project.
Specifically, COFEK sought documents detailing Nairobi's financial obligations, potential guarantees, and public liabilities associated with the 700,000 b/d refinery.
However, Mbadi said the petitioners failed to establish that the refinery project fell under the country's publicprivate partnership framework, adding that they also lacked sufficient legal grounds to order the disclosure of the requested documents.
"The refinery represents a commercial venture that does not provide for an eventual transfer of ownership to the Kenyan government," Mbadi said. "The authorities' support falls within standard investment facilitation mechanisms available to eligible private investors."
As a result, the country's Public-Private Partnership review panel dismissed the COFEK complaint.
On Sept. 30, Kenya's president William Ruto and Aliko Dangote, Africa's richest man and the builder of the 700,000 b/d Dangote Refinery in Nigeria, officially broke ground on the petroleum and petrochemicals refinery in Lamu county, which promises to upend East Africa's fuel market.
On Oct. 14, a Kenyan court revoked its earlier instructions requiring the Dangote Group to "maintain the status quo" at its proposed site in Lamu, following opposition from 133 local residents, who alleged the company had failed to provide proper notice and poses an undue threat to the local environment. In the Oct. 14 ruling, the judge found the complainants had failed to serve proper notice to Dangote, according to local media reports.

VENEZUELA DATA: Crude output edges up to 1.230 million b/d in October: preliminary data
Orinoco Belt crude production dips to 687,000 b/d
Output from western states up 12,000 b/d at 371,000 b/d
Venezuela's crude oil output averaged 1.230 million barrels per day in October, up from 1.218 million b/d in September, according to preliminary data from the Ministry of Hydrocarbons seen by Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, Nov. 3.
Production continues to be affected by prolonged power outages, especially in the west of the country, as well as delays in upgrading the infrastructure that supports oil operations, according to the report.
Western fields Overall output from fields in the western states of Zulia and
Trujillo averaged 371,000 b/d in October, up from 359,000 b/d reported for September.
Of that, fields operated by state-owned PDVSA in the west produced 71,000 b/d, more or less steady on the month, while production from joint ventures and under Hydrocarbon Productive Participation Contracts (CPPHs) rose to 300,000 b/d in October from 288,000 b/d in September.
The Petroboscan JV between PDVSA (60%) and Chevron (40%) produced 115,925 b/d, edging up from 115,307 b/d in September.
Petrozamora, a JV between PDVSA and North American Blue Energy Partners (NABEP), produced 106,583 b/d in October, up from the 98,369 b/d recorded in September.
Production at seven other fields in Zulia state awarded to NABEP -- Lagunillas Lago, Intercampo Norte, Tia Juana Lago, Lagomar, Rosa Medio, Lago Medio, and Lago 5 -- totaled 51,900 b/d in October, up from the September average of 51,493 b/d.
NABEP attributed the increase in production to the addition of equipment for repairing oil wells.

Active drillship utilization has reached at least 90%, with demand for sixth- and seventh-generation drillships expected to exceed 95% of available capacity by the end of 2027, Transocean management said, citing data from Fearnley Offshore.
Global drillship backlog has climbed to 237 rig-years, equivalent to approximately 2.5 years per unit, its highest level in more than a decade, the company said.
The strengthening market is beginning to change how operators secure offshore drilling capacity, with longer contracts and earlier commitments potentially improving pricing power for contractors, management said.
Average drillship contract duration has increased to approximately 380 days in 2026 from 245 days last year, said Roddie Mackenzie, chief commercial officer, during the company's Q3 earnings call, noting that the company's pipeline includes several opportunities lasting two to three years or longer.
Unlike the previous offshore investment cycle, which encouraged substantial new rig construction and multiyear commitments extending beyond five years, the current recovery is characterized by more disciplined capital allocation and limited fleet expansion.
"It's a very different cycle we're in, and obviously, the capital discipline being employed by our customers is poles apart from where it was in 2014," CEO Keelan Adamson said.
Management expects forward rig availability, rather than current utilization alone, to become an increasingly important determinant of contract economics.
Supply growth is also expected to remain constrained. Transocean said it would not reactivate cold-stacked drillships without contracts that guarantee acceptable returns, despite rising demand and tightening availability. Such reactivations require approximately 12-15 months, and management indicated that additional capacity was unlikely to return within the next year. "We are not in the habit, and we will not be creating the habit of plowing hundreds of millions of dollars into reactivations on questionable economics," Mackenzie said.
Exploration growth reshapes regional demand The expansion of exploration programs across West Africa,
India and Southeast Asia is expected to absorb additional rig capacity, potentially tightening availability in established offshore markets.
Transocean expects Africa's active deep-water fleet to grow from about 15 rigs to more than 20 over the next 18 months, supported by opportunities in Côte d'Ivoire, Mozambique, Namibia and Nigeria.
India could add as many as four drillships over the next two years as national oil companies expand offshore exploration and development under government initiatives aimed at increasing domestic production.
The company also identified seven to nine rig-years of potential demand across four or five opportunities in Southeast Asia, where only one deep-water rig is currently active.

Brazil is expected to remain a cornerstone of global deepwater activity, supporting a relatively stable fleet of 30-33 rigs over the next five years.
Recent discoveries by BP and Petrobras have reinforced expectations for additional exploration work, while the Mediterranean could require another two or three rigs as discoveries advance toward appraisal and development.
The growing geographic competition for drilling capacity is already prompting rig movements away from the US Gulf.
Management nevertheless expects US Gulf demand to remain strong over the longer term, particularly as operators develop Paleogene reservoirs requiring high-specification drilling units.
Energy security supports longer investment cycle National oil companies have emerged as a principal driver of
the offshore contracting recovery, accounting for about 60% of Transocean's year-to-date contract awards by backlog value.
Their investments increasingly reflect government efforts to strengthen domestic energy production and reduce exposure to international supply disruptions.
Mackenzie said geopolitical developments and concerns about energy security have accelerated a broader shift toward hydrocarbon investment that began approximately 18 months ago.
The company's recent $300 million contract with India's ONGC illustrates the urgency with which some national operators are securing scarce drilling equipment.
"Those that move quickest are the ones that are going to secure the assets, and those that don't are going to be disappointed," Mackenzie said.
International oil companies, meanwhile, remain focused on matching drilling commitments with approved exploration and development programs rather than responding to short-term movements in crude prices.
"They're justifying this on break-evens that are $50 and below," Mackenzie said. "They are not talking about justifying things at $80 or $90 a barrel."

Global product tanker rates are surging as geopolitical disruptions, strong refining margins and depleted fuel inventories drive demand for shipping capacity, Ardmore said Nov. 3, after reporting recent bookings at more than twice the levels achieved the prior quarter.
Ardmore's MR tankers have booked an average $57,600/d so far in November, with 45% of available days covered, more than double the $27,000/d earned during the third quarter.
"If you were to zoom in further, our recent bookings are north of $80,000 per day," CEO Gernot Ruppelt said during the company's Q3 earnings call.
The strength comes before the traditional winter market fully develops, with Ruppelt pointing to tight Atlantic product markets, elevated refining margins and shifting export flows from Asia as supportive factors.
"It feels like a winter market already before it is even winter," Ruppelt said.
The shift has been particularly pronounced given the recovery in petroleum flows alongside lingering inefficiencies in global trade patterns.
"Overall, the barrels are back, but the efficiency is not," Ruppelt said. "You, of course, now see product tankers continuing to connect these inefficient commodity markets in a very efficient way."

Inventory rebuilding adds demand Ardmore expects depleted petroleum product inventories to
provide another layer of tanker demand as refiners and importers rebuild stocks.
European jet fuel inventories have fallen to roughly half their level at the beginning of the year and are below their five-year range, according to data presented by the company. US gasoline and diesel stocks are also below their respective five-year lows.
Ardmore estimated that the decline in global product inventories represents more than 600 MR-equivalent cargoes, while moves by some governments to increase strategic reserves could further extend the restocking cycle.
