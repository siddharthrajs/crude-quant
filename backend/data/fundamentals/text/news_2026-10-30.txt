ExxonMobil eyes 2026 Permian oil output at 1.8 million boe/d
Three automated drilling rigs already on site
Studies underway for secondary recoveries
Chevron benefits from 25% less Permian capex

Soaring refining margins, strong trading fuel bumper quarter for BP
Upstream output holds flat at 2.2 mil boe/d
Refining margin nears $47/b on Gulf disruption
CEO 'pleased' with offers for North Sea business

ExxonMobil Corp. is aiming to end the current year with an oil production of some 1.8 million barrels/day of oil equivalent in Texas' Permian Basin, on the back of its primary recoveries, even as the company initiates work on secondary recovery techniques, CEO Darren Woods said Oct. 30.
ExxonMobil is the only operator in the Permian that is using automated drilling rigs, Woods said during the company's thirdquarter 2026 earnings webcast, adding that it currently has three such rigs on site that are delivering some of the strongest drilling performance while significantly increasing safety.
"We expect to expand their use to about 25% of our Permian fleet next year and approximately half by 2028, helping us continue to grow US production safely and at a lower cost," he said.
ExxonMobil holds a leading acreage position in the Permian Basin and plans to increase production to over 2.5 million boe/d by 2030 and beyond, the company said in May 2026, noting at that time it was maintaining a cost of supply of $30/b or less to grow its shale oil production in that basin.
Technology will continue to play a pivotal role in improving recoveries from an unconventional reservoir - like the Permian Basin - currently estimated at 6% to 8%, it said then.
For its part, ExxonMobil is now "well on the way" to improving recoveries by 50% by the end of the decade, the company said, noting that its proprietary lightweight proppant offers significant potential to increase recoveries.

BP kept upstream output flat in the third quarter and saw refining throughput dip following the divestment of its Gelsenkirchen refinery, but its profits nonetheless surged to multiyear highs in a quarter dominated by unprecedented refining margins amid the US-Iran war.
Posting its Q3 earnings on Oct. 30, the British company said it produced 2.2 million barrels/day of oil equivalent in the three months, in line with Q2 but down about 140,000 boe/d year over year due to Latin American portfolio adjustments, maintenance and Middle East disruptions.
That included 784,000 boe/d from its gas and low-carbon unit and 1.44 million boe/d from its oil production and operations division, BP said.
Refining throughput fell quarter over quarter from 1.47 million b/d to 1.4 million b/d after the sale of the Gelsenkirchen plant in Germany to the Klesch Group.
However, BP's refining indicator margin soared to $46.70/b, compared with $29.60/b in Q2 and just $15.80/b in the third quarter of 2025, as the Middle East conflict pushed up refined product cracks.
Upstream plant reliability and refining availability increased quarter over quarter, and its trading performance also improved from a high base in Q2. With extreme volatility in energy markets stemming from the Middle East conflict, European majors have benefitted significantly this year from their shift into trading.

ASEAN targets full ratification of petroleum-security pact before Nov summit: ACE
Six of 11 members ratify energy security pact Oil bill projected to reach about $369B after 2045 Emergency aid requires 10% supply shortfall

ASEAN aims to complete ratification of its renewed petroleum-security agreement before the bloc's November summit, paving the way for coordinated regional emergency responses during an energy crisis, ASEAN Centre for Energy Executive Director Razib Dawood said.
Six of ASEAN's 11 members have ratified the ASEAN Framework Agreement on Petroleum Security (APSA), with the remaining countries working through domestic approval procedures, according to the ASEAN Centre for Energy.
"The target is to fully ratify," Dawood told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, in an interview during Singapore International Energy Week 2026, held Oct. 26-30. He noted that member states aim to announce completion at the November summit.
APSA will take effect 90 days after ratification is formally deposited with the ASEAN secretary-general. The ASEAN Centre for Energy, the agreement's secretariat, would then finalize emergency-response protocols and drills to test how assistance would work during a supply disruption, Dawood said.
Signed in October 2025, the renewed pact will replace the 2009 agreement when it enters into force. It covers crude oil, petroleum products, and natural gas, establishing a framework for members to assist countries facing supply shortages.
Under the framework, members are to endeavor collectively to supply the equivalent of 10% of a distressed country's normal requirements on negotiated terms. Assistance remains voluntary and commercial, limiting the assurance of emergency cargoes.
The affected country must implement demand-reduction measures before requesting assistance in the event of a critical shortage. A critical shortage is defined as a shortfall of at least 10% of normal domestic requirements lasting at least 30 consecutive days.
Dawood said simultaneous supply pressures across ASEAN in 2026 highlighted this constraint, leaving countries with little spare fuel to offer their neighbors.
The arrangement would be better suited to responding when one or two countries face shortages and other members have surplus supplies, he said.
"We have to have some kind of immediate mechanism to cushion the disruption," Dawood said. "You have to secure the molecule."
The ASEAN Centre for Energy's ninth ASEAN Energy Outlook, launched Oct. 8, underscores the longer-term exposure. Under its baseline scenario, final energy consumption more than quadruples between 2025 and 2060, while the annual oil bill rises to about $369 billion after 2045 from about $165 billion in the late 2020s, in 2020 prices.

Strategic stockpiling Dawood said the bloc could explore shared strategic
stockpiling under APSA, although a regional initiative remains at the feasibility-study stage. Various models would need to be considered, and individual countries could pursue their own arrangements.
The APSA framework allows voluntary, commercial stockpiling, including joint arrangements among members, without establishing a pooled reserve.
Beyond emergency buffers, Dawood pointed to efficiency, fuel switching, alternative energy and regional cooperation as ways to reduce import dependence while supporting economic growth.
The ASEAN Centre for Energy's coordinated-transition scenario projects 2060 transport oil consumption at about 8 million b/d below the baseline, highlighting the potential for electrification and other measures to reduce fuel exposure.
With an expanding consumer market, manufacturing potential and opportunities for investment in energy infrastructure, the bloc has strong growth prospects, Dawood said, highlighting the region's pragmatism in realizing that potential. "We are ... a very predictable region," he said. "You can depend on long-term investment, long-term return."
Dawood said strengthening energy security would support economic expansion and the energy transition, while increased infrastructure investment would create jobs and contribute to growth. "It is not either-or. We can do all," he said.

US, Canadian flows South Korea's imports of US crude reached 13.13 million
barrels in September, up 17.5% year over year, KNOC data showed. Cumulative US shipments rose 19.1% to 144.23 million barrels during January-September, cementing the country's position as South Korea's second-largest crude supplier after Saudi Arabia.

Canadian crude arrivals rose to 1.73 million barrels in September, up 51.8% from August, taking January-September imports to around 12.6 million barrels, KNOC data showed. Demand for Cold Lake and Access Western Blend has strengthened because their heavy sour qualities make them useful substitutes for Basrah Medium, Arab Medium and Arab Heavy that are difficult to secure, the feedstock manager at the Ulsan-based refiner told Platts during a market engagement and Platts Market on Close (MOC) eWindow monitoring session on Oct. 29.
The growth in heavy sour Canadian crude shipments also supports South Korea's target of importing around 16 million barrels of Canadian crude in 2026, more than triple the 4.88 million barrels received in 2025.

Sinopec expects high crude, shipping costs to pressure Q4 refining: Executives
Crude premiums, freight and insurance costs may exceed $20/b in Dec
Jan-Sept crude throughput down 10% YOY
Oil product export margin above $150/mt
Sinopec expects international crude prices and freight rates to remain elevated in the fourth quarter, putting significant pressure on its refining business, company executives said during Sinopec's Q3 briefing conference call on Oct. 30.
"We believe uncertainty remains over navigation through the Strait of Hormuz, while there are also no signs of easing in some other geopolitical conflicts," CFO Shou Donghua said. "We expect the international crude and shipping markets in Q4 to reflect a combination of supply-demand dynamics and geopolitical premiums, keeping prices at elevated levels."
Sinopec's preliminary forecast showed that crude procurement premiums, freight and insurance costs would remain at $15/b or above in the fourth quarter and could exceed $20/b in December, Shou said.
By comparison, crude procurement premiums, freight and insurance costs fell to about $4.29/b in Q3 from a peak of $14.74/b in Q2 when the Middle East conflict broke out, Shou said, adding that the decline boosted EBIT by 20.7 billion yuan.
The freight for a 270,000 mt VLCC carrying crude from the Arab Gulf to China averaged $245.27/mt as of Oct. 29 in Q4, according to data from Platts, part of S&P Global Energy.
Under China's current oil product pricing mechanism, introduced in 2013, the government adjusts retail ceiling prices for gasoline and gasoil every 10 working days based on a basket of benchmark crude prices, reportedly including ICE Brent, NYMEX WTI and DME Oman, with the components weighted according to China's crude import profile.
Market sources said freight and insurance were set at a combined $5/b in the formula, Platts reported.

Nigeria's Seplat says on course for upper end of oil output guidance
Indigenous company reactivates 41 idle wells
Repays $100M debt from ExxonMobil deal
Yoho offshore field remains shut for repairs
Seplat Energy is on course to achieve its 2026 oil and gas production target of 155,000 barrels/day of oil equivalent after seeing output rise 7% year over year in the first nine months, the indigenous Nigerian producer said late Oct. 29.
In early 2026, Seplat set a full-year production target of 145,000-155,000 boe/d, having completed its acquisition of ExxonMobil's onshore and shallow water assets in December 2024.
Over the first three quarters of the year, production has averaged 144,999 boe/d, the company said in its statement, while Q3 production was 155,801 boe/d, up 13% year-on-year.
The company said its performance had been bolstered by reactivating wells abandoned by the US supermajor, with the Nigerian producer adding 33,700 b/d of oil and 176 MMcf/d of gas from 41 idle wells.
"I am delighted to report another quarter of robust production growth, averaging over 155,000 boe/d in Q2 2026, which puts us firmly on track to meet the upper half of our full year guidance," Seplat CEO Effiong Okon said.
"We have clear line of sight to continued growth that will be underpinned by operational excellence as offshore gas production comes back online."
The ExxonMobil deal gave Seplat a 40% stake in four Nigerian oil producing blocks -- OMLs 67, 68, 70 and 104 -- as well as the Qua terminal and the Bonny River terminal and Natural Gas Liquids plant.
Seplat said that strong oil output and higher global commodity prices during the period under review had allowed it to repay the $100 million balance of the debt it raised to acquire the ExxonMobil projects on Oct. 27.

Cameroon's Sonara inks fuel supply deal with Nigeria's Dangote
Deal a response to Iran war fuel price impact
Ensures stable flow of refined products
Dangote has upended regional fuel market
Cameroon's national refiner, Sonara, has signed a refined products supply deal with Nigeria's Dangote refinery, as the huge privately owned plant outside Lagos continues to reshape the African fuel market.
In a statement posted on its Facebook page late Oct. 29, the Societe Nationale de Raffinage of Cameroon said the tripartite agreement -- which also includes Nigerian downstream player MRS Oil and Gas Co. -- is a direct response to the Middle East conflict's impact on fuel prices.
"This agreement is of fundamental importance in the current context of Middle East tensions, the impact of which on the global economy and particularly on our country's economy are undeniable, notably through rising petroleum product costs and real risks of supply shortages," Sonara said in the release signed by its general manager, Harouna Bako.
The agreement will ensure "stable and sustainable" fuel supply to Cameroon, the company said. Aliko Dangote, Africa's richest person and the owner of the refinery, will travel to Cameroon in the coming days to formalize the agreement, it added.
Sonara did not specify how much product Dangote had agreed to supply. Representatives for the state-backed firm and Dangote did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

Since coming online in 2024, the Dangote refinery has upended the fuel market in Nigeria and the wider West Africa region, with its ability to meet domestic demand and export products to international markets. The plant at one point became Europe's top jet fuel supplier during the US-Iran war.
The plant hit its planned 650,000 barrels/day of processing capacity in February 2026 before upgrading by a further 50,000 b/d in recent months.
Dangote hopes to double that to 1.4 million b/d by 2029 through the addition of a second crude distillation unit, financed in part by a hugely popular initial public offering, which recently concluded.
Like many of its regional neighbors, Cameroon is an oil producer with limited refining capacity, making it heavily reliant on imported fuel.
Curbs on flows through the Strait of Hormuz following the Iran war have sent product cracks and freight costs soaring in recent months, prompting fuel crises in several African countries.
Cameroon has imported around 11,000 b/d of fuel this year on average, down slightly year over year, with Nigeria and China its biggest suppliers, according to ship-tracking data from S&P Global Commodities at Sea.
The compares with Nigeria's total fuel exports for 2026 of 253,000 b/d, with Togo, the UK and Cote d'Ivoire its top export destinations.
Platts assessed gasoline on a FOB West Africa basis at $1,126/ mt Oct. 29, down from a recent peak and up from roughly $680/ mt before the start of the Middle East conflict.

Olmeca drives throughput increase The Olmeca refinery in Dos Bocas, Tabasco, accounted
for roughly half of the year-over-year increase in crude processing.
