Water levels on the Rhine river slumped to fresh record lows for the second time in a week, Oct. 1, the German water authority reported, preventing fuel tanker deliveries from making their way inland and dashing hopes for a rapid rebound in traffic ahead of peak winter demand levels.
After a summer of record temperatures and droughts across Europe, low water levels have become an increasingly serious threat to the continent's busiest river, typically a vital conduit for energy, metals and chemicals to reach inland markets. Earlier this week, a car carrier ship was reported to have run aground at Dusseldorf harbor, while elsewhere on the continent, a slump in Danube river levels recently revealed lost WWII-era shipwrecks and forced power plants to shut down.
Official readings from the German water authority WSV showed water levels in Kaub, the Rhine's shallowest point, had dropped 6 centimeters below its 113 cm benchmark level, surpassing a previous all-time low of minus 2 cm against the baseline threshold recorded on Sept. 29. The WSV's benchmark rate, or "zero" reading, varies in different segments of the river, subject to water depth.
Falling water levels at the Kaub pinchpoint in Germany follows reports of record low water levels in parts of the lower Rhine, which runs from near Bonn in Germany to the border with the Netherlands. China's clean oil product exports to dip sharply over Oct 1-7 despite new quota allocation
Refineries miss deadline amid late quota release
Oct clean oil product exports set to fall MOM
China's oil product exports are expected to dip sharply during the Oct. 1-7 National Day Golden Week holiday as the late release of the third batch of export quotas left many exporting refiners with only a brief customs-clearance window on Sept. 30, four refining sources with Sinopec and PetroChina told Platts Oct. 1.
The quotas were issued at around 3 pm local time Sept. 30, while customs offices closed at 5 pm, leaving exporters roughly two hours to complete the necessary procedures, forcing many to delay loading cargoes until after the holiday, the sources added.
However, an export specialist at a Sinopec refinery told Platts that the company would still load three refined products cargoes for export to overseas markets during the Oct. 1-7 Golden Week holiday.
"Thanks to all the preparations, I secured customs release just one minute before customs closed," the source said, adding that many other refiners were unable to obtain customs clearance in time.
An export specialist at a PetroChina refinery said it had not received any administrative order to halt exports or suspend cargo loadings, suggesting the expected decline in oil product exports was primarily due to customs clearance bottlenecks rather than policy restrictions.
It would not be surprising if policy restrictions were introduced at any time, given the potential for a tightening domestic market, two of the refinery sources said.
Oct exports On Sept. 26, market sources estimated China's clean oil
product exports at around 3.85 million mt in October, including shipments to foreign destinations, Hong Kong, and Macau, as well as jet fuel supplied for bonded bunkering at the country's international airports.
The volume would be down from 4.36 million mt in September, as per estimates from market sources, and from the 4.58 million mt reported by Chinese customs for the month, due to improving domestic demand in October and potentially lower throughput from tight feedstock supplies.
China's oil product exporters had been eager for a fresh round of export quotas after strong shipments in August and September depleted much of their remaining quota availability.
The four refining sources from Sinopec and PetroChina said the third batch was released Sept. 30, although details were not immediately available.
Platts reported Sept. 24 that the third batch allocation was likely to be smaller than the corresponding batch issued last year, implying a volume of less than 8 million mt.

China allocated 8.395 million mt of clean oil product export quotas in its third-round issuance in mid-September 2025.
In this year's first two batches, the exporting oil companies were awarded 32 million mt of quotas, up 0.6% from the corresponding batches issued in 2025.
The exporting oil companies are PetroChina, Sinopec, CNOOC, Sinochem, China National Aviation Fuel, Zhejiang Petroleum & Chemical, and Norinco.

INTERVIEW: India needs coastal crude blending hubs to supplement SPRs, build resilience
Costal hubs to store, blend crude closer to import terminals
SPRs to remain vital component of India's energy security
Gulf oil producers investing to develop alternative routes
India needs to consider developing coastal crude blending and stabilization hubs to complement its strategic petroleum reserves, enabling the country to manage a more diverse and volatile crude supply slate while giving refiners greater flexibility to respond quickly to shifting trade flows, supply disruptions and market conditions, Shrikant Madhav Vaidya, former chairman of Indian Oil Corp. Ltd., said, Sept. 30.
The facilities could allow India to receive, store and blend different crude grades closer to import terminals. Investments in port connectivity, storage, blending systems and inland distribution networks could also help move crude efficiently from coastal hubs to refineries, Vaidya told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy.
"SPRs will continue to remain an important pillar of India's energy security. However, the next increment of national resilience may come less from building larger crude stockpiles and more from strengthening strategic flexibility -- through refinery adaptability, coastal crude blending hubs, diversified procurement and agile logistics," Vaidya said.
"The real lesson from the Strait of Hormuz crisis is not that SPRs have become less important. It is that the world for which they were conceived has changed. India's energy security strategy must evolve accordingly," he added.
In its first phase, India established SPRs at three locations with a combined capacity of 5.33 million metric tons: 1.33 million mt at Visakhapatnam, 1.5 million mt at Mangalore and 2.5 million mt at Padur in Karnataka. In the second phase, Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Ltd. plans to add 6.5 million mt of storage capacity at two sites: 4 million mt at Chandikhol in Odisha and 2.5 million mt at Padur.
The SPRs cover about 9.5 days of net oil imports, while state-run oil companies hold crude oil and petroleum products in storage equivalent to 64.5 days of net imports. According to petroleum ministry data, this brings India's total storage capacity for crude and petroleum products to roughly 74 days of net imports -- below the 90-day minimum required of International Energy Agency member countries.

Rethinking energy security strategy Vaidya said SPRs were conceived many decades ago, when
the Middle East dominated internationally traded crude; the North Sea had yet to emerge as a major source; the US was a major importer rather than a producer; Brazil had not become an offshore oil power; Canada's oil sands were commercially insignificant; Guyana had not discovered oil; and Russian crude was neither freely traded nor readily available to Asian buyers.
"In that world, SPRs were the right answer. If crude supplies were interrupted, countries could survive by drawing down emergency stocks until trade resumed. Building large underground reserves became the logical insurance policy against geopolitical disruption," Vaidya said.
He added that the oil industry was moving from an era of "crude of choice" to one of "crude adaptability."
"Future competitiveness will depend not only on refinery complexity but also on refinery flexibility -- the ability to process a wider range of crude oil while maintaining operational stability, product quality and profitability," Vaidya said.
Future investments should therefore focus on expanding crude-processing flexibility, strengthening logistics and establishing dedicated coastal hubs for crude blending and stabilization, he added.
"During geopolitical disruptions, crude arriving from different parts of the world may vary significantly in quality. Scientifically blending these streams near import terminals can create refinery-compatible feedstocks, allowing India to procure opportunistically from a much wider range of suppliers while maintaining operational stability and product quality. Such facilities should be viewed as strategic national assets rather than merely refining infrastructure," Vaidya said.
Overcoming future challenges Vaidya said recent tensions in West Asia had once again
turned the Strait of Hormuz into the world's most closely watched energy chokepoint. As the possibility of disruption grew, many feared that India -- which imports more than 88% of its crude oil requirements and receives nearly 40% of those imports through the Strait of Hormuz -- would face severe fuel shortages.
"That did not happen. There was no nationwide panic-buying, no prolonged disruption in the supply of petrol, diesel, aviation turbine fuel or LPG and no major interruption to economic activity. The government, Indian oil companies, shipping agencies and the country's refining sector responded with remarkable agility," Vaidya said.
He added that the Strait of Hormuz would remain India's single most important maritime energy chokepoint for the foreseeable future. A substantial share of India's crude imports originates from producers in the Arabian Gulf, making the strait strategically indispensable.
"However, an equally important development is underway. Gulf producers themselves are investing to progressively reduce their dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and increasingly develop alternative routes," Vaidya said.

"These developments will not eliminate the strategic importance of the Strait of Hormuz. A prolonged disruption would still have profound implications for global energy markets. However, they do indicate that the concentration of risk is likely to diminish progressively as alternative export infrastructure develops," he added.
Vaidya said energy security could no longer be viewed as a static concept based solely on geography and crude storage. Instead, it must increasingly be built on a combination of SPRs, diversified sourcing, refinery flexibility, logistics resilience and the ability of producers and consumers to adapt to a changing geopolitical landscape.
"Today, crude oil is globally traded, geographically diversified and remarkably fungible. The challenge has shifted from merely securing crude to efficiently processing whatever crude the market makes available," Vaidya said.
Premasish Das, executive director for oil analytics at S&P Global Energy CERA, said China and the US have complemented their SPRs with extensive commercial storage and crudehandling infrastructure, while Japan and South Korea have built flexible logistics systems that allow them to respond quickly to supply disruptions.
Hence, for India, the next stage of energy security may require a multipronged approach. SPRs will remain important, but investments in blending hubs, commercial storage, refinery flexibility, diversified crude sourcing, better port and pipeline connectivity and faster operational and commercial decisionmaking can together create a more resilient system, Das added.

India reduces windfall tax on diesel, jet fuel exports
Diesel tax drops to 16 Rupees/liter
Jet fuel levy cut to 10.5 Rupees/liter
India cut the windfall gains tax on exports of diesel and jet fuel and retained the previous level for gasoline, according to a gazette notification from the Ministry of Finance Oct. 1, providing some relief to domestic refiners at a time when earnings are challenged by renewed geopolitical tensions in the Middle East.
The new rates will be applicable for a fortnight from Oct. 1, according to the notification.
The new rate of the windfall tax -- also known as special additional excise duty (SAED) -- along with road and infrastructure cess on the export of diesel will be 16 Rupees/liter, down from 20 Rupees/liter, it said.
The new rate for jet fuel will now be 10.5 Rupees/liter, down from 15 Rupees/liter earlier, the notification read.
The tax on overseas sale of petrol or gasoline will remain at 0.5 Rupee/liter for the fortnight until Oct. 15, it added.
India introduced the export duty in March amid the Middle East conflict, with rates being reviewed periodically based on global crude and product prices.

Kazakhstan targets 7 million mt/year of crude via BTC pipeline
Azerbaijan agrees to boost transit to 2.7 million mt
BTC pipeline operates at half capacity now
Kazakh crude quality poses no blend issues
Kazakhstan aims to increase its crude supply through the Baku-Tbilisi-Ceyhan pipeline, which runs from Azerbaijan to

Turkey, to 7 million metric tons per year, Kazakhstan's energy ministry director of oil transportation and refining, Daulet Arykbayev, told Platts on Oct. 1.
"Azerbaijan is definitely ready [to increase flows through the BTC], they are very interested," Arykbayev said on the sidelines of the KIOGE conference in Almaty, Kazakhstan, and added that the ongoing discussions with the Azerbaijani side are "purely commercial," focused on volumes and discounts for transportation services.
"Overall, they have expressed a willingness to increase volumes to 2.2 million mt/year (44,000 barrels/day), and subsequently to 2.7 million (54,200 b/d). Looking further ahead, there is even potential to reach 7 million (140,600 b/d) within the next few years," Arykbayev said.
Kazakhstan exported 1.2 million mt (24,100 barrels/day) of oil via the BTC in 2025 and around 700,000 mt (14,000 b/d) in the first half of 2026, according to KazTransOil.
Turkey and Azerbaijan are interested in loading the BakuTbilisi-Ceyhan pipeline to its full capacity of 1 million barrels/day, Turkish and Azeri energy ministries told Platts in September. The BTC pipeline currently carries only around 500,000 b/d-600,000 b/d, according to the Turkish energy minister Alparslan Bayraktar.
To achieve the goal, Turkey wants to attract oil from Central Asia and the Turkic countries, the Turkish minister said in September, without clarifying the potential suppliers.
Kazakhstan's Arykbayev said that the quality of Kazakh crude should not become a problem for the extension of transit through Azerbaijan.
"They have their own blend -- Azeri Light -- and supplies of our oil dilute it slightly. However, there is a broader trend at play: they have a great deal of idle capacity, so it ultimately comes down to the price. Filling that idle capacity can only be beneficial for the country," Arykbayev said.
Azerbaijan's Socar did not respond to Platts' request to comment on the possibility of higher BTC transit flows.
Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, last assessed Azeri Light CIF at $119.255/b on Sept. 30, while the CPC Blend CIF Augusta Suezmax was assessed at $105.18/b on the same day.

ConocoPhillips weighs sale of Norway assets, UK's Teesside terminal
US major evaluates unsolicited offer
Pumped 121,000 boe/d in Norway in 2025
Operates Greater Ekofisk Area in North Sea
ConocoPhillips is weighing whether to sell its Norwegian oil and gas business, and the UK's Teesside oil terminal, after receiving an offer, it said Oct. 1.
In a statement on its website, the US major said it was "evaluating a potential sale of its Norway business and Teesside asset after receiving an unsolicited offer."
The company's portfolio in the region is based around the Greater Ekofisk Area, while it also holds non-operated stakes in the Heidrun, Troll, Aasta Hansteen, Alvheim and Visund projects.

Light sweet Ekofisk crude is one of three Norwegian grades that help comprise the Dated Brent benchmark, the backbone of the global physical oil trade. Dated Brent was last assessed at $120.44/b on Sept. 30.
The company said in its statement that "this evaluation is consistent with the company's disciplined strategy to optimize and high-grade its portfolio. If we do not receive an offer that meets the company's expectations for value, ConocoPhillips will retain the assets."
It wasn't immediately available for further comment on the potential sale, nor did it specify which company had made the offer.
ConocoPhillips is one of the last major international companies still operating offshore Norway, with the sector now dominated by local players and state-backed Equinor.
In its 2025 annual report, the US firm said it pumped 121,000 barrels per day of oil equivalent in Norway last year, including 63,000 b/d of crude and 330 million cubic feet/day of natural gas.
The Greater Ekofisk Area lies offshore Stavanger in the Norwegian North Sea and comprises five producing fields. Its crude is exported to the Teesside terminal -- also operated by ConocoPhillips -- while its gas is exported to Emden in Germany.
As a result, the company also holds a 35.1% interest in the 220-mile Norpipe Oil Pipeline system to Teesside and a 40.25% stake in the UK crude stabilization and NGLs processing facility.
Meanwhile, Ekofisk traded at a $18.25/b premium to Dated Brent on Sept. 30, according to Platts data, having surged in value since the US-Iran war trimmed crude flows from the Middle East. Platts is part of S&P Global Energy.

As a significant oil and gas importer, Turkey is significantly exposed to high energy prices. It also faces supply risks linked to Western sanctions imposed on key suppliers Russia and Iran.

US executives see Persian Gulf export disruptions lasting until 2027
Executives expect diesel crack spreads to remain elevated
Production grows despite cost inflation squeeze

Diesel concerns In the survey's special questions section, nearly half of all
respondents -- 48% -- said the spread between diesel prices and crude prices would require more than four quarters to return to 2025 levels. The equivalent figure for gasoline was 36%, suggesting executives see diesel tightness as a more entrenched structural problem than elevated crude.
Survey comments pointed to several reinforcing factors. One producer said domestic refining capacity had become a bottleneck for consumer fuel markets, while an oilfield services executive said Russian diesel exports were unlikely to resume in the near term and suggested China could increasingly influence global refined-product supply. Another respondent described diesel as "the mother's milk of the economy" and said broader economic impacts were only beginning to emerge.
The persistence of wide diesel crack spreads, if sustained, would have significant implications for refinery run rates, crude selection and product trade flows, particularly on routes linking the US Gulf Coast, Europe and Asia where middle distillate balances have already tightened sharply since the onset of the Iran conflict.
High prices and looming elections have in recent weeks prompted US policymakers -- including US President Donald Trump -- to propose a ban on US diesel exports. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright has downplayed that possibility and said the administration is looking to keep "a little more diesel in the United States" without using a "blunt hammer" that could negatively impact the broader economy.
A complete diesel export ban would create a massive domestic surplus requiring US refiners to reduce crude runs by nearly 2 million barrels/day, over 10% of current capacity, while causing a reduction in US gasoline supply and higher prices, S&P Global CERA analysts Debnil Chowdhury, William O'Neil and Brian Stetter forecast.

Waterborne gasoline and blendstock imports into the US West Coast reached 2.56 million barrels in September, narrowing their annual deficit to 18.5% as a late-month buying spree by California refiners revitalized inbound trade, according to US Customs data from Oct. 1.
The modest decline from the 3.14 million barrels imported in September 2025 marks a decisive turnaround from mid-summer lows, when trans-Pacific arbitrage economics collapsed and foreign deliveries into mainland California temporarily dried up.
By late September, tightening regional inventories and scheduled autumn refinery turnarounds reopened the arbitrage window. Mainland California ports in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Martinez absorbed 2.26 million barrels--accounting for over 88% of all imports for the month.

Brussels confident CBAM will survive Russia's WTO challenge
WTO panel to review carbon border tax on Russia's request
EU will engage with panel but won't negotiate directly with Moscow
US, BRICS nations add to growing chorus of CBAM critics
The European Commission said it is confident its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism fully complies with World Trade Organization rules, as it prepared to defend the landmark climate trade measure before a formal dispute panel established at Russia's request.
"We are confident that CBAM is designed in full compliance with WTO rules," a commission spokesperson told Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, Sept. 30, adding that Brussels would engage with the panel proceedings but would hold no direct negotiations with Moscow.
The WTO's Dispute Settlement Body agreed Sept. 25 to Russia's request to establish a panel to review CBAM and what Moscow describes as an "alleged export subsidy" under the EU Emissions Trading System, opening a new legal front in the battle over the bloc's climate policy.
Under the EU's CBAM, importers must buy certificates matching the carbon cost they would have paid under EU rules. Most exporting countries either lack a carbon price or set one well below the EU ETS level, which Brussels says justifies the mechanism. CBAM's definitive phase began Jan. 1, 2026, targeting imports of steel, aluminum, cement, hydrogen, fertilizers and electricity.
Brussels pushes back The EU's position on Russia's legal standing was unambiguous.
"The EU sees no legitimacy in these proceedings initiated by Russia," the spokesperson told Platts. "Russia's recourse to WTO rules over the EU's regulatory, climate-oriented, environmental

measure is unjustifiable in the context of its blatant violations of the most fundamental rules of public international law through its war of aggression against Ukraine."
Despite that position, Brussels said its commitment to the multilateral rules-based system was the sole reason it would participate. "There will be no engagement or negotiations with Russia itself," the spokesperson said.
Russia launched its WTO dispute in May 2025, calling CBAM and the ETS "highly trade-restrictive and discriminatory mechanisms established under the pretext of climate policy." As a major EU supplier of steel, aluminum and fertilizers, Moscow argues the mechanisms breach multiple WTO agreements.
In a push to level the playing field for EU producers, CBAM requires importers of carbon-intensive goods to buy certificates corresponding to the carbon price that would have been paid had those goods been produced under EU carbon pricing rules.
Global response to CBAM CBAM has drawn sharp reactions globally. In August, US
Ambassador to the EU Andrew Puzder called it protectionism disguised as climate policy. An EC spokesperson pushed back, saying CBAM applies equally to all countries based on verified emissions and imposes little or no obligation on lowcarbon goods.
China has raised concerns within the WTO but stopped short of a formal complaint. BRICS nations have repeatedly condemned such measures as discriminatory. The EU insists CBAM creates a level playing field and erects no trade barriers.
The dispute reflects a stark carbon price gap. EU permits are about seven times more expensive than Chinese compliance prices. Platts assessed EU Allowances for December 2026 at 85.06/mtCO2e ($95.88/mtCO2e) on Sept. 30, against China's compliance allowance at Yuan 94.99/mtCO2e ($14.07/mtCO2e) on Sept. 24, according to the Shanghai Environment and Energy Exchange.

Rhine water levels fall to new record low, stalling fuel logistics ...from page 1

The historically low water levels have stopped most tanker traffic from navigating the Upper Rhine, which traverses southern Germany to the neighboring Swiss market, putting extra pressure on alreadytight fuel stocks affected by the US-Iran war. Swiss government data showed that heating oil costs hit their highest since 2022 in September, climbing 8% from the previous month's average.
According to data from Sportbarge, a Dutch shipping and bunker monitoring company, only two specialist chemicals barges, Raadsman and Millora managed to transit the Kaub crossing Oct 1, but shipping was otherwise at a standstill. Only a few captains and barge companies are willing to risk the voyage in such extreme conditions, with regular vessels having to wait for WSV levels closer to plus 60-70 cm, or a minimum of 173 cm at Kaub, before attempting to make the voyage.
Refinery exposure The river acts as an important trade route for several key
refineries, notably Shell plc's 190,000 barrels/day Godorf refinery at its Rheinland complex and 316,000 b/d Karlsruhe/Miro plant, as well as the major production hubs on the coast in the Netherlands. According to Sportbarge estimates, loadings from both Shell refineries were down by a third week over week on Sept. 28, keeping pressure on product flows in already-tight markets.
Shell did not immediately respond to request for comment. As a rule of thumb, barges typically need to cut their cargo loads by up to 75% when WSV readings drop below 75 cm, keeping product movement limited and pushing up freight rates. The barges that sail the Rhine typically carry volumes of between 2,000 metric tons and 5,000 mt, but those still sailing the shallower segments have been forced to slash loads. Barge rates from

Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp hub to Karlsruhe are currently lingering at to 200/mt, almost ten times their rate in January.
"The majority of barges that are now in the Upper Rhine stay there for loadings from the Miro Refinery for local depot supply, such as Strasbourg and Basel, but also with low tonnage," said Spotbarge CEO Sebastiaan Kosman, noting that trade flows would only be able to return gradually as water levels rise. The WSV forecasts a slow recovery in levels in the coming days, but sees readings staying below 10 cm until Oct. 5.
As peak winter heating oil season approaches, traders expressed concern that lasting disruption could hamstring inland trade, with limited capacity to shift products to rail and pipeline logistics. Traders have already faced challenges rerouting gasoline via pipeline due to Swiss quality restrictions, and have butted up against capacity restrictions on regional rail networks.
Arne Lohmann Rasmussen, chief analyst at Danish energy investment advisory Global Risk Management, estimated that fully replacing Rhine river transportation would take roughly 3,000 extra road tankers daily to manage oil product deliveries alone.
"Low Rhine water levels are the last thing the German economy needs," Rasmussen said. "German industry is already struggling with high energy prices, US tariffs and increasingly tough competition from Asia. A new logistical chokepoint adds higher costs and more uncertainty to an economy already under pressure."
